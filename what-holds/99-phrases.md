# 99 Phrases to Thrive and Flourish
### Sayings, scripts, and linguistic tokens for Justin, drawn from history and human needs

---

## Why These 99

A proverb that has survived for centuries is compressed experience: thousands of people met the same problem, and this sentence is what lasted. Everyday scripts like *"Can you show me how?"* are smaller tools, but they do the same job. They give you words ready before you need them.

The 99 are sorted by **human need**. The groupings draw on Abraham Maslow's hierarchy (1943), Manfred Max-Neef's fundamental human needs (1991), and the NVC needs list you're already learning. They're also matched to what your own archive shows is hardest for you:

- asking for help
- staying in the present
- speaking honestly without exploding or going silent
- owning mistakes
- handling money, paperwork, and time
- not reading every glance as a judgment

**Nine sections of eleven.** Each entry gives the phrase, where it comes from, and when to use it. Where an attribution is uncertain or popular but wrong, it's marked.

**How to learn them:** one phrase a day for 99 days, or one section a week for nine weeks. Say each one out loud, write it once by hand, and use it once in real life that day. A short starter set is at the end.

## I. Presence and Ground
*Need: safety, orientation, peace. The spoke "the one I live with."*

**1. Be here now.**
*Origin:* Title of Ram Dass's 1971 book; a core teaching across meditation traditions.
*Use it when:* your mind is in the past or the future and your body is standing on gravel.

**2. Wherever you go, there you are.**
*Origin:* Old saying, made famous by Jon Kabat-Zinn's 1994 book on mindfulness.
*Use it when:* you're tempted to believe a new town, job, or walk will fix the inside.

**3. The map is not the territory.**
*Origin:* Alfred Korzybski, *Science and Sanity* (1933).
*Use it when:* a label, file, diagnosis, or price starts feeling like the whole truth about something.

**4. Don't believe everything you think.**
*Origin:* Modern saying, rooted in cognitive therapy and Buddhist practice.
*Use it when:* a thought arrives with total certainty and a racing heart.

**5. Name it to tame it.**
*Origin:* Psychiatrist Dan Siegel. Research shows that putting feelings into words calms the brain's alarm response.
*Use it when:* you feel flooded. Say "This is shame," or "This is fear."

**6. Know thyself.**
*Origin:* Inscribed at the Temple of Apollo at Delphi, ancient Greece.
*Use it when:* you're about to adopt someone else's identity, plan, or framework as your own.

**7. The unexamined life is not worth living.**
*Origin:* Socrates, in Plato's *Apology* (399 BCE).
*Use it when:* you need to remember why this archive is worth writing.

**8. Look to one star.**
*Origin:* Your own archive, from a Native American prayer book found at the BYU library: in your greatest fear, look to one star.
*Use it when:* everything is dark and you need one fixed point.

**9. There's nothing to fix. There's nothing wrong.**
*Origin:* Mooji's teaching, from your Advaita training.
*Use it when:* you're trying to repair yourself like a broken machine.

**10. This too shall pass.**
*Origin:* A Persian Sufi fable of a king's ring, carried into English by Edward FitzGerald (1852) and Abraham Lincoln (1859).
*Use it when:* a bad hour feels permanent. It applies to good hours too, so savor them.

**11. Keep calm and carry on.**
*Origin:* British government poster, 1939.
*Use it when:* something goes wrong at work and you need to keep your hands steady.

## II. Body, Breath, and Rest
*Need: physical well-being. The spokes "body" and "food."*

**12. Health is the first wealth.**
*Origin:* Ralph Waldo Emerson, *The Conduct of Life* (1860): "The first wealth is health."
*Use it when:* deciding between spending on your body (the septoplasty, teeth, sleep) and spending on things.

**13. A sound mind in a sound body.**
*Origin:* Latin *mens sana in corpore sano*, from the Roman poet Juvenal (around 100 CE).
*Use it when:* your thinking is spinning. Move your body first.

**14. HALT: Hungry, Angry, Lonely, Tired.**
*Origin:* A recovery-community slogan from the 12-step tradition.
*Use it when:* you're about to make a big decision or say something sharp. Check those four first.

**15. Hunger is the best sauce.**
*Origin:* Cicero, then Cervantes in *Don Quixote* (1605–1615).
*Use it when:* you need to remember that simple food, eaten at mealtimes, is enough. (In high school you skipped lunch, so eat it.)

**16. It is solved by walking.**
*Origin:* Latin *solvitur ambulando*, attributed to Diogenes; used by St. Augustine.
*Use it when:* a problem won't untangle. Go for a walk, a short one, and come home.

**17. Rest is not idleness.**
*Origin:* John Lubbock, *The Use of Life* (1894): "Rest is not idleness… it is by no means a waste of time."
*Use it when:* clock-mind tells you that lying down is falling behind.

**18. Sleep on it.**
*Origin:* English proverb; the Latin *in nocte consilium* means "in the night, counsel."
*Use it when:* you're about to quit a job, confront someone, send a message, or spend money.

**19. Exhale longer than you inhale.**
*Origin:* Modern breath physiology (studies of the slow exhale and the "physiological sigh"). It calms the nervous system.
*Use it when:* fight-or-flight hits. Breathe through whatever side is open. You don't need a perfect airway.

**20. Make your bed.**
*Origin:* Admiral William McRaven's 2014 University of Texas commencement speech.
*Use it when:* the day feels shapeless. One finished task starts momentum.

**21. Early to bed and early to rise makes a man healthy, wealthy, and wise.**
*Origin:* Benjamin Franklin, *Poor Richard's Almanack* (1735), from an older English proverb.
*Use it when:* the night pulls you toward screens.

**22. Motion is lotion.**
*Origin:* Physical therapists' saying.
*Use it when:* your back or ankle is stiff and you want to stay still.

## III. Asking for Help
*Need: support. Your archive names this as your biggest lifelong gap: "I never asked for help."*

**23. Can you help me?**
*Origin:* Plain English, and the most underused sentence in your archive.
*Use it when:* you've been stuck for more than fifteen minutes.

**24. Could you show me how?**
*Origin:* The apprentice's question, as old as crafts.
*Use it when:* you're at work and pretending you already know.

**25. I don't know how to do this yet.**
*Origin:* Psychologist Carol Dweck's research on the power of *"yet"* (*Mindset*, 2006).
*Use it when:* not knowing feels like failing. *Yet* keeps the door open.

**26. Smart people ask questions.**
*Origin:* Your seventh-grade math teacher, Mr. Milam: "Stupid people just sit there, smart people ask questions."
*Use it when:* pride tells you to stay quiet.

**27. He who asks is a fool for five minutes; he who doesn't ask stays a fool forever.**
*Origin:* Often given as a Chinese proverb; the exact source is uncertain.
*Use it when:* the embarrassment of asking feels bigger than the cost of not asking.

**28. Ask, and it shall be given you.**
*Origin:* Matthew 7:7, King James Bible (1611).
*Use it when:* you assume no one will help. Most people say yes to a clear, kind request.

**29. Many hands make light work.**
*Origin:* John Heywood's collection of English proverbs (1546).
*Use it when:* you're carrying something alone that could be shared.

**30. No man is an island.**
*Origin:* John Donne, *Devotions upon Emergent Occasions* (1624).
*Use it when:* you start isolating, like the closed bedroom door in high school.

**31. What would you do in my shoes?**
*Origin:* English idiom ("in someone's shoes," 1700s).
*Use it when:* you need advice but don't know how to ask. It invites wisdom without pressure.

**32. Let me check with someone I trust first.**
*Origin:* A modern decision-making script, echoing the old counsel of elders.
*Use it when:* a big idea, deal, or plan feels urgent. Urgency is a reason to slow down.

**33. Thank you for helping me.**
*Origin:* Universal gratitude.
*Use it when:* always, and soon, ideally the same day.

## IV. Kinship and Connection
*Need: belonging, love, community. The spoke "other people."*

**34. Love thy neighbour as thyself.**
*Origin:* Leviticus 19:18, repeated by Jesus in Mark 12:31.
*Use it when:* a neighbor, coworker, or stranger feels like an obstacle.

**35. Do unto others as you would have them do unto you.**
*Origin:* The Golden Rule (Matthew 7:12). Confucius taught the reverse form around 500 BCE: "Do not impose on others what you do not wish for yourself" (*Analects* 15:24).
*Use it when:* you're not sure what the right move is.

**36. I am because we are.**
*Origin:* *Ubuntu*, a Southern African (Nguni Bantu) philosophy of shared humanity.
*Use it when:* you start believing you have to make it entirely alone.

**37. Be kind, for everyone you meet is fighting a hard battle.**
*Origin:* Often misattributed to Plato. Most likely Ian Maclaren (Rev. John Watson), 1897.
*Use it when:* a customer, manager, or passerby is short with you.

**38. Pay it forward.**
*Origin:* Lily Hardy Hammond (1916): "You don't pay love back; you pay it forward."
*Use it when:* you remember Capitán, Shiloh, Tim Epps, Greg Fort, Christy, and Nadine. You can't repay them, but you can pass it on.

**39. Count your blessings.**
*Origin:* Johnson Oatman Jr.'s hymn of the same name (1897).
*Use it when:* comparison or scarcity takes over. Name three, out loud.

**40. A friend in need is a friend indeed.**
*Origin:* From the Roman poet Ennius, via medieval English.
*Use it when:* deciding which friendships to invest in, and what kind of friend to be.

**41. How are you doing, really?**
*Origin:* Everyday English with one extra word.
*Use it when:* you want a real conversation instead of a social script.

**42. I appreciate you.**
*Origin:* Modern American English.
*Use it when:* your mother, Rich, Nick, Quinton, or a coworker does something kind, even something small.

**43. There's no place like home.**
*Origin:* John Howard Payne's song "Home, Sweet Home" (1823), later *The Wizard of Oz*.
*Use it when:* you're tempted to set off walking again. Housing is the base for every other freedom.

**44. Blessed are the peacemakers.**
*Origin:* Matthew 5:9, the Sermon on the Mount.
*Use it when:* you can either escalate or calm a situation.

## V. Honest Speech and Conflict
*Need: honesty, understanding, respect. Your archive names "ruinous empathy" and "manipulative insincerity" as old patterns.*

**45. When ___ happened, I felt ___, because I need ___. Would you be willing to ___?**
*Origin:* Marshall Rosenberg's Nonviolent Communication (1960s onward).
*Use it when:* you're upset with someone. Fill in the blanks. Leave out blame.

**46. Care personally, challenge directly.**
*Origin:* Kim Scott, *Radical Candor* (2017). This is the quadrant you've been reaching for.
*Use it when:* you need to say something hard to a coworker or manager.

**47. Speak the truth in love.**
*Origin:* Ephesians 4:15.
*Use it when:* honesty is about to become a weapon.

**48. A soft answer turneth away wrath.**
*Origin:* Proverbs 15:1.
*Use it when:* someone raises their voice. Lower yours.

**49. Seek first to understand, then to be understood.**
*Origin:* Stephen Covey (1989), adapted from the Prayer of St. Francis: "grant that I may not so much seek… to be understood as to understand."
*Use it when:* you're about to defend yourself before hearing the other person out.

**50. We have two ears and one mouth, so we can listen twice as much as we speak.**
*Origin:* Attributed to the Stoic philosopher Zeno of Citium by Diogenes Laërtius.
*Use it when:* you notice you've been talking for a long time, especially out loud to yourself.

**51. When angry, count to ten before you speak; if very angry, to a hundred.**
*Origin:* Thomas Jefferson, his "Canons of Conduct" (1825).
*Use it when:* a salesman provokes you. (You told one you wanted to punch him.)

**52. Let your yes be yes, and your no be no.**
*Origin:* Matthew 5:37; James 5:12.
*Use it when:* you're tempted to agree to please someone, then quietly not follow through.

**53. No is a complete sentence.**
*Origin:* Modern saying, popularized by Anne Lamott and others.
*Use it when:* someone pushes you toward something that isn't good for you, including a scammer.

**54. I hear you.**
*Origin:* Modern English for reflective listening (Carl Rogers's client-centered approach, 1950s).
*Use it when:* someone needs to feel heard before anything gets solved.

**55. That's fair.**
*Origin:* Everyday English.
*Use it when:* someone makes a good point against you. Granting it costs nothing and builds trust.

## VI. Accountability and Repair
*Need: integrity, trust, self-respect.*

**56. I was wrong. I'm sorry. How can I make it right?**
*Origin:* The three-part apology, central to restorative justice and to the amends steps (8 and 9) of the 12-step tradition.
*Use it when:* you've hurt someone. All three parts, no "but."

**57. My mistake. I'll fix it.**
*Origin:* Workplace English.
*Use it when:* something goes wrong at work. It ends blame quickly and moves to action.

**58. The buck stops here.**
*Origin:* The sign on President Harry Truman's desk (1940s).
*Use it when:* you notice yourself blaming the system, your father, or the church for something that was your choice.

**59. To err is human; to forgive, divine.**
*Origin:* Alexander Pope, *An Essay on Criticism* (1711).
*Use it when:* someone else messes up, and when you do.

**60. Honesty is the best policy.**
*Origin:* English proverb, recorded around 1600; later associated with Franklin.
*Use it when:* a small lie looks easier. Remember the lie to Joe at Cougar Pizza.

**61. Actions speak louder than words.**
*Origin:* English proverb, 1600s.
*Use it when:* you've apologized for the same thing twice. The third apology is a changed action.

**62. Don't burn your bridges.**
*Origin:* From military retreats, when armies burned bridges behind them so they couldn't go back.
*Use it when:* you're about to walk away from a job, roommate, or friend in anger. Leave the door open.

**63. Keep your word.**
*Origin:* The oldest social contract there is.
*Use it when:* making any promise, big or small. If you can't keep it, say so early.

**64. Leave it better than you found it.**
*Origin:* Robert Baden-Powell, founder of Scouting: "Try and leave this world a little better than you found it." You earned your Eagle under this.
*Use it when:* leaving a room, a car, a job, a campsite, or a conversation.

**65. Do a good turn daily.**
*Origin:* The Scout slogan.
*Use it when:* the day feels self-centered. One small act of help turns it around.

**66. Forgive yourself, and do better.**
*Origin:* A modern synthesis of self-compassion research (Kristin Neff) and accountability.
*Use it when:* guilt is stopping you instead of changing you.

## VII. Work and Craft
*Need: competence, contribution, purpose.*

**67. Chop wood, carry water.**
*Origin:* Zen saying, traced to the Chinese layman Pang Yun (8th century): "My miraculous power: carrying water and chopping wood."
*Use it when:* moving cars on the lot feels beneath you. Ordinary work, done fully, is the practice.

**68. Work is love made visible.**
*Origin:* Kahlil Gibran, *The Prophet* (1923).
*Use it when:* you're looking for meaning in a job that doesn't explain itself.

**69. Measure twice, cut once.**
*Origin:* Carpenters' proverb, centuries old.
*Use it when:* paperwork, a car move, a message, or anything that's hard to undo.

**70. Whatever is worth doing is worth doing well.**
*Origin:* Lord Chesterfield, letter to his son (1746).
*Use it when:* you're tempted to coast, the way you did through senior year and college.

**71. Showing up is most of the battle.**
*Origin:* Adapted from Woody Allen's line that "eighty percent of success is showing up."
*Use it when:* you don't feel like going to work, court, or an appointment. Go anyway.

**72. Be prepared.**
*Origin:* The Scout motto (Baden-Powell, 1907).
*Use it when:* the night before a shift, a court date, or a trip.

**73. A stitch in time saves nine.**
*Origin:* Thomas Fuller, *Gnomologia* (1732).
*Use it when:* official mail arrives. Open it today and handle it this week.

**74. Rome wasn't built in a day.**
*Origin:* Medieval French proverb; in English in Heywood's collection (1546).
*Use it when:* progress feels too slow at thirty-seven.

**75. Little by little, one goes far.**
*Origin:* Spanish proverb, *poco a poco se va lejos*. You spoke Spanish for two years in Sonora.
*Use it when:* you need patience with a long project like the debt, the septoplasty, or saving.

**76. Done is better than perfect.**
*Origin:* Modern workplace maxim.
*Use it when:* perfectionism is stopping you from finishing, like the history capstone.

**77. Could you tell me what you'd like me to do first?**
*Origin:* Workplace English.
*Use it when:* a manager gives you a lot at once. Asking for the priority shows competence, not weakness.

## VIII. Money, Paperwork, Time, and Home
*Need: security, order, autonomy. The spokes "time" and "home."*

**78. Pay yourself first.**
*Origin:* George S. Clason, *The Richest Man in Babylon* (1926): "A part of all you earn is yours to keep."
*Use it when:* payday arrives. Set aside something, even $10, before spending.

**79. A penny saved is a penny earned.**
*Origin:* An older English form, "a penny spar'd is twice got" (George Herbert, 1640), later associated with Benjamin Franklin.
*Use it when:* a small purchase tempts you, like vape cartridges once did, or apps.

**80. If it sounds too good to be true, it probably is.**
*Origin:* Modern American consumer proverb.
*Use it when:* any online romance, deal, or offer asks for money or gift cards.

**81. Trust, but verify.**
*Origin:* The Russian proverb *doveryay, no proveryay*, made famous by Ronald Reagan in the 1980s.
*Use it when:* someone online or in person makes claims you can't check.

**82. Get it in writing.**
*Origin:* English law's Statute of Frauds (1677) required certain agreements to be written down.
*Use it when:* a landlord, employer, or court promises you something.

**83. Read before you sign.**
*Origin:* A basic rule of contract law.
*Use it when:* any form, lease, plea, or agreement is put in front of you.

**84. When in doubt, don't.**
*Origin:* Proverb, with versions going back to Benjamin Franklin.
*Use it when:* you feel pressured into a quick yes.

**85. Hope for the best, prepare for the worst.**
*Origin:* English proverb, 1500s.
*Use it when:* planning a trip, a budget, or a week.

**86. A place for everything and everything in its place.**
*Origin:* A 19th-century household proverb.
*Use it when:* your room starts filling up. You cleared one dead man's belongings; keep it clear.

**87. Time is the coin of your life.**
*Origin:* Attributed to Carl Sandburg: "It is the only coin you have, and only you can determine how it will be spent."
*Use it when:* scrolling, or anyone wants your hours for free.

**88. First things first.**
*Origin:* A 12-step slogan; also the title of a 1994 Covey book.
*Use it when:* everything feels urgent. Pick one thing.

## IX. Resilience, Perspective, and Meaning
*Need: hope, growth, meaning.*

**89. Fall seven times, stand up eight.**
*Origin:* Japanese proverb, *nana korobi ya oki*.
*Use it when:* you remember 2015–2024. You stood up every time.

**90. A journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step.**
*Origin:* Laozi, *Tao Te Ching*, chapter 64 (literally, "begins beneath one's feet").
*Use it when:* a goal feels impossibly far. You've walked from Tillamook to Marin, so you know this one in your legs.

**91. The best time to plant a tree was twenty years ago. The second-best time is now.**
*Origin:* Often called a Chinese proverb; the source is uncertain.
*Use it when:* you feel you're "last among your peers."

**92. Comparison is the thief of joy.**
*Origin:* Commonly attributed to Theodore Roosevelt, though that's unverified.
*Use it when:* you measure yourself against other people's age, wages, or marriages.

**93. Run your own race.**
*Origin:* Runners' saying. You ran a 3:33 marathon by running your own pace.
*Use it when:* someone else's timeline starts setting yours.

**94. Some things are within our power, and some are not.**
*Origin:* Epictetus, *Enchiridion*, chapter 1 (around 125 CE).
*Use it when:* you're angry at a system. Sort what you can act on from what you can't.

**95. God, grant me the serenity to accept the things I cannot change, the courage to change the things I can, and the wisdom to know the difference.**
*Origin:* Reinhold Niebuhr (around 1932–33); adopted by Alcoholics Anonymous in the 1940s.
*Use it when:* you need the whole of #94 in a single breath. Use whatever word for "God" works for you.

**96. Never assume malice when busyness or a mistake explains it.**
*Origin:* Adapted from Hanlon's razor (1980), which says "stupidity" instead of busyness or a mistake.
*Use it when:* a glance, a delay, or a bureaucratic letter feels personal.

**97. Not my circus, not my monkeys.**
*Origin:* Polish proverb, *nie mój cyrk, nie moje małpy*.
*Use it when:* a stranger's behavior, a coworker's drama, or a passerby pulls at you to react.

**98. Bloom where you are planted.**
*Origin:* Attributed to St. Francis de Sales (17th century).
*Use it when:* you want to escape the job, the town, or the room. Grow here first, then choose.

**99. I'm here now. I exist. I continue.**
*Origin:* Your own living language, the refrain from every chapter of this archive.
*Use it when:* always. It's yours, and it belongs at the end of any list of words worth keeping.
