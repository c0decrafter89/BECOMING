## 1. Executive Summary

1. **You write in four distinct registers** and switch between them without warning: plain record, interpretive dictation, coined high-concept language, and live field notes. The plain record and the field notes are your most precise writing. The interpretive dictation is where most of the words go.
2. **Your style runs on a small set of master metaphors**: the *automaton*, *sky city*, *ground*, stored *epinephrine*, and the *box or net*. These five images carry most of your explanation of your own life. They are strong and they are overworked.
3. **Your main framing device is retroactive prophecy.** Songs, dreams, overheard remarks, and library books are read as foretelling what came later ("I basically foretold What would come to pass"). It gives the story shape, and it hides how much of that shape is added afterward.
4. **Time in the archive runs on four clocks at once**: the remembered past, the moment of dictation, the hindsight verdict, and a prescribed developmental schedule. The last one is the most powerful and the least examined. You measure yourself against the README's age brackets, and you usually find yourself behind.
5. **Uncertainty is everywhere, and it's often deleted downstream.** There are about 129 explicit markers of uncertainty or forgetting, plus 56 instances of "I guess." When the material is tidied (by the reference record, or by me), those hedges tend to disappear and guesses harden into facts.
6. **The biggest single gap between observation and interpretation is claims about other people's minds**: that the mission president was "testing" you, that Wil "never forgot" your laugh, that institutions "want you to fall into a hole and die." These are written in the same grammar as observed facts.
7. **Your clearest and most moving writing comes from inhabiting the past in the present tense**, in the child-voice passages of `here-now.md` and the scene lists of `skeleton.txt`. That's the voice to build on.

## 2. The Four Registers

### Register A: The Plain Record
*Direct syntax. Who, what, where, when.*

Short declarative sentences, lists, dates, proper names, places. It appears in `skeleton.txt`, `chronological-life-account.txt`, and in short runs inside the dictations.

> "Ms hoffmeister was my teacher for 6th grade."
> "I drew an elk in 4th grade. I still had glasses in fourth grade."
> "Potosí / Oruro / Tarija / sucre / 5/9 departments"
> "worked at butcher shop til January 2023. in April of the 2023 i got Lasik vision correction surgery from nvision in Beaverton."

The baptism catalog in `the-open-field` (Jesús Tenorio, Patricia, Chano, Felipe Bello, Luis Ornelas, Oswaldo and Enrique) is pure Register A, and it's the most concrete stretch of that 18,000-word file.

**What it does well:** it can be checked, it's quick to read, and it's emotionally restrained in a way that lets the reader feel things. **Weakness:** it's rare. Outside the reference record it shows up in short bursts and then gives way to Register B, usually within a sentence or two.

### Register B: Interpretive Dictation
*Run-on, associative, and self-commenting. This is the dominant register.*

Long unpunctuated speech-to-text runs that fold memory, interpretation, theory, judgment, and side remarks into one stream. Most of the 7,300-word Storm Years file and nearly all of the Open Field and True Reckoning files are written this way.

> "I literally lived in that bean bubble for many years … it's like I substrated like a mirror world exactly like the regular world that I just you know tricked everybody into thinking that I was like grounded in real life I did everything like everybody else but truly I I was a different fact"

Its signatures:
- **Borrowed theory used as everyday language:** id, ego, and superego; Radical Candor's four quadrants (19 uses of *manipulative / ruinous empathy / obnoxious / radical candor*); "biopolitical" and "panopticon" (10).
- **Your own technical vocabulary:** *substrate / substrated on* (19), *sky city* (14), *root dream*, *penalty* (9).
- **Hedges:** "I guess" (56), "like," "kind of," "basically."
- **Chains of association:** one sentence in the Storm Years file walks from eating lunch to sex to togetherness to employment to economy to growth to perpetual reproduction ("Eden is a ground for sex and sex is a ground for for togetherness and tether togetherness is the ground for employment…"). It's a sorites chain: each link sounds reasonable, and together they claim a cosmology.

**What it does well:** honesty in motion. It catches thinking as it happens, including the parts you'd edit out. **Weakness:** observation and verdict get the same weight, so a reader (including you, later) can't tell which sentences are memory and which are theory.

### Register C: The Coined High-Concept Name
*Capitalized compound titles and polished, essayistic prose.*

> "The Untouchable-Bubble Enclosure: The Dissociative-Armor Citadel"
> "The Panoptic Chrono-Compliance Cell: The Industrial Sunrise Inversion Chamber"
> "The Step-Wife Automaton: The Default-Programmed Social Script"
> "The Maternal-Lyric Panopticon: The Bandmate-Projection Archive"
> "Psi-Crest-Open-Sis"

And whole pasted paragraphs in a very different voice:

> "Under chronic cultural friction and scarcity, this concept is trapped in a defensive paradigm of perpetual warfare…"
> "Look, I'm saying it straight out—right from inside that steel trap they built out of a dictionary and a prescription pad."
> "Standard usage treats the sovereign breakthrough of non-ordinary reality as an enemy of the state and the self."

These read as generated or heavily edited by AI. The em-dashes, balanced clauses, and "Title: Subtitle" pattern don't match the dictation, and in `The-True-Reckoning` you say you crafted the Bill of Intention with AI.

**What it does:** it names a state, gives it the dignity of a concept, and holds it at a distance. **Risk:** the name can stand in for the description. "The Dissociative-Armor Citadel" tells a reader what to conclude without showing what it looked like at a school lunch table. The pasted paragraphs also sound more certain than the hesitant voice around them, and they can quietly overwrite it.

### Register D: Live Field Notes
*Observation in the present moment, inserted mid-memory.*

> "two people have walked past me one person is rode their bike past me a black girl and a Red Riding Hood cave is is running eastbound … there's a guy in an American flag shirt with a lot of cargo"
> "I'm just on Crestview and 7th exploring this little jaunt"
> "I'm getting out of a ionic 5 … walking back to the main lot moving another ionic 5"
> "standing with my back to the garage door with Patrick's new used Subaru outback with a luxless a lightless American flag waving in the wind"
> the helicopter circling overhead during the Nictehai passage, counted lap by lap

**What it does well:** this is the most purely observational writing in the archive: specific, sensory, and in the present tense. It anchors the dictation in a body and a place. **Risk:** it often slides straight into a verdict on the people observed ("everybody is so drawn of energy it's frightening," "gutless wanton-minded Street roaming addicts"). That's the same jump from observation to judgment that runs through the memories.

## 3. Recurring Tropes and Metaphor Systems

Each cluster below shows the approximate count, typical phrasings, and what job the image does in the story.

### 3.1 The Automaton (≈23, plus related images)
*automaton, social automaton, robot, autopilot, puppet strings, sleepwalking (rendered by dictation as "sonambulance" or "san ambulance"), "operating system," "battery."*

> "I constructed an automaton identity as a permanent declaration"
> "someone else had the puppet strings and I was just merely going along in the motions"
> "my id ego and superego are are without like a operating system or user interface"

**Function:** it explains why you went through the motions without feeling, and why other people bought it. It's your most coherent self-image, and it's validated inside the archive by Wil's song "You may be a robot but I'm not a toy."
**Development:** it grows from a child's survival strategy into a computing metaphor, then into the operating system of Mormonism ("Mormon OS integration"), and finally into your own *Unified Operating Structure*. You've kept the machine metaphor and changed who writes the software. That's worth noticing: the Bill of Intention is the automaton image turned around, not abandoned.

### 3.2 Sky City, the Bubble, and the Mirror World (≈14 "sky city," ≈14 "mirror," plus "bubble" and "cloud")
> "frustrated on sky City fooling everybody that I was grounded"
> "I literally lived in that bean bubble" (from the cartoon song "I Love Beans": "I sit in my own little cloud")
> "mirror mirror magic mirror"

**Function:** a place-metaphor for dissociation: high, sealed, and reflective. The mirror moves from a figure of speech (2002–2008) to something you took literally (Douglas's mirror in 2018, the temple's facing mirrors). That shift from figurative to literal is one of the archive's most important arcs, and it isn't flagged in the text.

### 3.3 Ground (≈42) and Root Dream
*ground, grounded, true ground, ground ground, root dream, mother Earth, substrate.*

**Function:** sky city's opposite and the archive's moral north. Being *grounded* is the stated goal. "Ground ground stand witness" works almost as an invocation.
**Pattern:** "ground" carries several meanings at once (physical earth, emotional stability, social standing, metaphysical reality) and slides between them inside a single sentence. The repetition "ground ground" reads like a reach for a word you don't have yet.

### 3.4 Stored Epinephrine and Fight-or-Flight (≈16)
> "I just further capacitated The epinephrine's surge storage that I had from from a child"
> "I at a unconscious level I think that the epinephrine is the end all be all of my wealth"

**Function:** a physiological explanation for emotional states. It ties present anxiety to childhood (your parents' fighting, the abuse at five).
**Pattern:** it's presented as mechanism ("stored," "capacitated," "surge storage"), which gives an interpretation the authority of biology. It may well be a useful model. Stylistically, it's still a metaphor.

### 3.5 The Box, the Net, and the Legal Hallucination (≈12 "box," ≈16 "legal," ≈10 "biopolitical / panopticon")
> "biopolitical box"
> "legal hallucination"
> "the beast of 666 does not permit travel between cities"

**Function:** a metaphor for systems. Institutions are containers and traps. This cluster carries the most emotional heat and the least observational detail. Systems are described as having intentions ("they probably want you to fall into a hole and die") in grammar identical to an observed fact.

### 3.6 Religious and Ritual Vocabulary (≈58 "Mormon," ≈17 "Satan," ≈9 "penalty")
*signs, tokens, penalties, priesthood, covenants, Zion, Satan, celestial kingdom, "living in my sins forever."*

**Function:** the language you grew up in, kept as a lens even after you left the faith. You use it both straight ("I believed I was in Satan's power") and ironically ("I gave him the first penalty"). The archive never fully separates those two uses, so a reader can't always tell sincerity from satire. The incident at work, where the gesture was read as a death sign, is that same ambiguity happening off the page.

### 3.7 Lying (≈44)
> "lying to myself about lying to myself about lying to my betrothed"
> "a big fat golden lie"
> "real … r e e l"

**Function:** the archive's governing confession. The recursive phrasing ("lying to myself about lying to myself") is a signature device. It performs the infinite regress it describes, and it ends any attempt to work out what was actually true at the time.

### 3.8 Shadow and Fantasy (≈23 "shadow," ≈15 "fantasy")
Jungian *shadow* and the related "fantasy / Fantasyland / Disney / Mickey Mouse" set.

**Function:** shadow names what you hid. Fantasy names the alternative world you lived in. Disneyland literally frames your marriage, from the honeymoon to the divorce, and you explicitly call it "the Disney permission slip."

### 3.9 Sex and Shame (≈69)
Present in every chapter.

**Function:** the archive treats the experience at age five as the origin point that explains most of what follows: quitting soccer, the laugh with Wil, the redness in your face, Legos, the marriage, the porn, the trial run at a gay identity. This is the deepest narrative structure in the archive, a single cause with many effects. Section 7 comes back to it.

## 4. Framing Devices

### 4.1 The Developmental Schedule
The README's age brackets (First Knowing 9–12, Storm Years 13–19, Open Field 18–29, True Reckoning 30–45) are more than a table of contents. They work as a yardstick:

> "now as a 30-45 year old I'm going thru my storm years. what I should've been preoccupied with at 13-19 I wasn't and am doing that work now."
> "I'm halfway through it with only 6 years left to go"
> "coming in virtually last place among my peers making 18 bucks an hour"

**Effect:** the schedule turns a life into a series of deadlines you're behind on. It's the archive's quietest source of shame, because it looks neutral. The brackets come from an outside template. They aren't facts about you.

### 4.2 Retroactive Prophecy
Over and over, an earlier sign is read as a foretelling:
- Wil's "The Storm" ("I didn't listen I I was oblivious to these these foretellings")
- the man at church saying "stay the course" ("it was like a foreshadowing")
- the library book about a man with many personalities ("I basically foretold What would come to pass")
- the dream of the rope in the pit ("the dream like I feel like I'm still having now")
- *Big Fish*: "where you see in adolescents how you're going to die as as an adult"

**Effect:** it gives a chaotic life the shape of a story with omens, and that's a classic memoir move. The cost is that it can make the outcome feel like fate rather than a series of choices and circumstances.

### 4.3 The Refrain
> "I digress I continue I'm here now" (≈8 "digress," ≈15 "I continue / I exist," ≈26 "here now")

**Effect:** your strongest device. It interrupts a spiral, resets to the present, and starts again. It works like a stanza break in a long poem. It's also the most direct link between your writing and your meditation practice (Mooji: "here now").

### 4.4 Closing Formulas
> "no comment" (≈9) · "yada yada yada" (≈10) · "bang bang" (≈7) · "that's all I have to say about that" · "enough said"

**Effect:** these shut a topic down. "No comment" often follows the most charged material: the gravedigger who moved into your room, the hospitalization in Colorado, your father. They mark where the story deliberately goes quiet. That's useful to know: these phrases are a map of what you haven't written yet.

### 4.5 Media as Interpretive Key
*Charlie and the Great Glass Elevator* · *The Pagemaster* ("look for the exit") · *The Lion King* ("remember who you are") · Mr. Magoo · *Big Fish* · *Coraline* · *Pirates of the Caribbean* · *Inside Out*, *Split*, *Glass* · *Little Nicky* · Disney's "if you wish upon a star" · Snoop, Bob Marley, Binary Star, Die Antwoord, Dylan, Cash, the Beatles.

**Effect:** in a family where movies were "practically sacred," films become your parables. They're vivid, quick shorthand. They also tend to replace a description with a reference: "I kind of became Mr. Magoo" stands in for a scene.

### 4.6 Borrowed Diagnostic Grids
Radical Candor's quadrants, Freud's id/ego/superego, NVC feelings-and-needs, the Wheel's six spokes, "biopolitics."

**Effect:** you sort experience into a borrowed matrix. Each grid gives order. Used together, they compete, and none of them is your own.

### 4.7 Inline Feelings-and-Needs Tags
> "I'm nervous needing emotional safety"
> "she was shocked needing consideration"
> "I'm self-conscious needing normalization or just like belonging"
> "the mind plays very nitpicky needing emotional safety meeting assurance needing trust meeting quality"

These look like NVC practice, either typed in or inserted from a list. **Effect:** they're the archive's most direct emotional statements, but they're grafted onto sentences rather than grown in them, so they can read as labels stuck on after the fact. Notice also that some are pinned to *other people* ("she was shocked needing consideration"). That's a guess about someone else's inner state written as a tag.

## 6. Temporal Structure

### 6.1 Four Clocks
1. **Then:** the remembered event ("in 7th grade I went to Brent Wong's birthday party").
2. **Now:** the dictation moment (the park bench, the bike, the car lot, the garage at sunset).
3. **Hindsight:** a verdict on then from now ("now see it was all based on a lie," "looking back," ≈10 explicit markers, many more implied).
4. **Schedule:** where you "should" be by your age (section 4.1).

The dictations hop between all four within a paragraph, often within a sentence: "and so I'm on alder right now oh look Chad Wright house for sale … where was I right right what river."

### 6.2 Tense Drift
Past events slip into present tense mid-sentence ("I go out with f****** elder Moreno … he thought I was the s***"). That's normal in speech, and it gives immediacy. In `here-now.md` the present tense is deliberate and it works.

### 6.3 The Counterfactual Mood (≈29)
> "if I would have been wiser and more present I would have hung out with him"
> "I should have married" (Nictehai, then immediately caught: "I'm substrated on … my dad who always says he should have married my mom's roommate")
> "I would have ask for help then. when you're supposed to get help."

**Effect:** counterfactuals carry grief and regret. The Nictehai passage is a rare case of the text catching its own pattern in real time. It recognizes the "should have married" line as your father's script. That self-correction is a model worth copying.

### 6.4 The Future, Mostly in Abstractions
Past events are rich in names and places. The future shows up mainly as systems and intentions: "the Bill of Intention," "the way of the wheel," "this chapter only gets better," "a walking business," "a Maverick as a truck driver." **Insight:** the future is under-described in concrete terms: what a specific Tuesday would look like.

### 6.5 Uncertainty Markers (≈129 + 56 "I guess")
> "I don't know," "not sure," "I can't remember," "I forgot" (≈89)
> "pretty sure," "probably / prolly," "I want to say" (≈40)

**Effect:** the dictations are honest about the limits of memory. That's an asset. It's mostly lost when the material is summarized. For example, the reference record says "served roughly 10 days in Washington County (Dallas, OR) jail," while the dictation says Wasco County, and the summaries disagree on the dates.

| Undercurrent | Where it shows | How it's expressed |
|---|---|---|
| **Shame** | Sex, the age-five memory, the bucket in the backyard, the redness in your face | Confession, then deflection ("no comment," humor) |
| **Anger** | Your father, the church, institutions, passersby | Profanity, system metaphors, verdicts on strangers |

| **Fear / unsafety** | Shelters, cops, the roar, the bus stop | Physiological language (epinephrine, heartbeat), flight |

**Key pattern:** **gratitude and grief come out in plain language, while shame and anger come out in theory.** When you're moved, you describe. When you're hurt, you explain.

**The single-cause structure.** The archive tends to trace large stretches of life back to one source: the abuse at five, your parents' fighting, your father's "chemical imbalance" lesson, or Mormonism. Each may truly be important. As a narrative choice, though, a single cause shrinks the role of everything else: luck, other people's choices, substances, money, your own decisions on a given day. Notice how often a sentence moves from a present event to "because I s******** at 5 years old." The link may be real, but the sentence asserts it rather than showing it.

## 8. Direct Observation vs. Subjective Interpretation

### 8.1 A Seven-Level Scale
To help you see the layers, here's a scale from most checkable (A) to least (G):

| Level | Name | Test | Example from the archive |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | Present observation | Could a camera record it now? | "a guy in an American flag shirt with a lot of cargo" |
| **B** | Recalled fact | Could a record or witness confirm it? | "I got Lasik vision correction surgery from nvision in Beaverton" |
| **C** | Recalled perception | Was it your sensory experience? | "it was loud in a silent, supernatural way" |
| **D** | Own motive or feeling, in hindsight | Is it about your inner state, judged later? | "I think secretly I hated myself that's why I pushed her away" |
| **E** | Another person's mind | Does it claim what someone else thought or intended? | "he never forgot this and was always subtly probing me" |
| **F** | System or cosmic claim | Does it assign intent to an institution or the universe? | "they probably want you to fall into a hole and die" |
| **G** | Verdict | Is it a moral judgment of self or others? | "I was a terrible person at that time" |

A and B are **direct**. C is **direct but subjective**. D through G are **interpretation**, increasingly so further down. All of them belong in a memoir. The problem is only when they look the same on the page.

### 8.2 Worked Example 1: 9/11 (`skeleton.txt`)

> "Ms hoffmeister was my teacher for 6th grade." → **B**
> "we stopped everything and watched the news for 9/11 coverage." → **B**
> "that morning my stepdad was taking me to school." → **B**
> "I didn't remember where my brothers were." → **B** (an honest gap)
> "I just remember being in his room, my parents room and watching the news broadcast." → **B / C**
> "it wasnt scary." → **C**
> "but it was loud in a silent, supernatural way." → **C**, poetic

**Verdict:** almost entirely A through C. One striking image, earned by the plain sentences before it. This is the archive's best model of the balance.

### 8.3 Worked Example 2: Wil and the Book (`the-storm-years/memories-1.txt`)

> "we flipped thru the book and eventually came to the solo male masturbation picture." → **B**
> "we laughed together." → **B**
> "but his laugh was genuine." → **E**
> "my laugh was macabre and hiding something." → **D**
> "he never forgot this" → **E**
> "and was always subtly probing me, testing me boundaries for sexuality" → **E**
> "all he could deduce was that I was probably a social automoton." → **E**, stacked on E
> "he wrote a song called 'you may be a robot but I'm not a toy'." → **B**
> "Wil became my spiritual mom in a sense" → **G / F**, a symbolic reading

**Verdict:** two facts (the book, the song) support a long chain of claims about what Wil thought. The story may be right. As written, though, a guess about Wil's mind reads with the same certainty as the song title. A version true to what you know would mark the inference: *"Years later I think he noticed. Then he wrote a song…"*

### 8.4 Worked Example 3: JFK and the Hospital (`the-open-field/memories.md`)

> "I bought a one-way ticket to New York" → **B**
> "I lived in the airport for like 3-4 days" → **B**, hedged
> "I walked out onto the highway … on the wrong side" → **B**
> "when the paramedics asked me if I wanted to see a mental health person I thought it was just like counseling" → **B / D**
> "it was the beginning of the nightmare of the subjugation of of your autonomy for economic output with psychiatric oversight" → **F**
> "they don't care about the history of your family upbringing … all they care about is is doing their job which is labeling you" → **E / F**
> "I was acting in a manner that would attract me a sexual partner in in the ward" → **D**, unusually candid
> "they like these people are all f***** up … and I'm good in the denial of the madness" → **D / G**, self-aware

## 10. Actionable Insights

### 1. Tag your layers
When revising, mark each sentence with a level from section 8.1. You don't need to delete anything. Just make the levels visible. A light format:

> **[B]** We flipped through the book and laughed. **[D]** My laugh was hiding something. **[E?]** I think Wil noticed.

### 2. Write in two columns
For any key memory, use two headings: **What happened** (A, B, C only) and **What I make of it** (D through G). Write the first column first and keep it under 150 words. Your 9/11 passage already does this naturally.

### 3. Turn mind-reading into observation
Every time a sentence says what someone else thought, felt, or intended (Level E), rewrite it as what they *did or said*, then add your reading as a reading. "He was testing me" becomes "The waitress dropped the menus, I didn't pick them up, and he looked at me. I think it was a test."

### 4. Ground system claims in one specific scene
Before writing "the biopolitical box" or "the legal hallucination," write one concrete scene: the 7-Eleven parking lot, the storage-unit camera, a court date. Let the reader reach the system-level conclusion from the scene. If no scene exists, that's worth knowing.

### 5. Keep a personal lexicon
Your coinages (*sky city*, *automaton*, *substrate*, *penalty*, *root dream*, the capitalized compounds) are a real private language. Write a short glossary entry for each, defining it once in plain words, so each use points to something specific. Decide which ones are yours and which came in through AI paste-ins, and mark the paste-ins.

### 6. Use the child's present tense on purpose
Choose one memory from each chapter and write it the way `here-now.md` is written: present tense, through your eyes at the age you were, no hindsight allowed. This is your strongest instrument.

### 7. Quarantine the counterfactuals
Gather the "I should have / if I would have" sentences into one section per chapter, titled something like *Roads Not Taken*. That respects the grief without letting it interrupt every scene. Check each one, as you did with Nictehai, for whose voice it really is.

### 8. Test single causes
When a sentence links an event to the age-five memory, your parents' fighting, your father, or the church, list two other plausible contributing factors next to it. Keep the link if it still holds. This makes the most important causal claims in the archive stronger, not weaker.

### 9. Treat the closing formulas as an index
Search for "no comment," "yada yada," "enough said," and "that's all I have to say about that." Each one marks a door you closed. List them. They're the unwritten chapters.

### 10. Separate field notes from memory
When dictating while walking or working, start present observations with a marker such as `[NOW]`, and return to the memory with `[THEN]`. Your refrain ("I digress, I continue, I'm here now") already does this by ear. Making it visible keeps both the field notes and the memory clean.

### 11. Describe the future concretely
For each future intention (truck driving, the walking business, the Wheel), write one ordinary scene: where you are, what time it is, what you're holding, who's there. Concrete futures are easier to walk toward than abstract ones.

### 12. Keep the hedges in the record
When you or a tool summarizes your dictation, carry over "I think," "I want to say," and "I'm not sure." Your uncertainty is accurate. Deleting it makes the record less true, not more.
