## Prologue — Here Now

It is the autumn of 2026. I am thirty-seven years old. I live in a single room in Hillsboro, Oregon — a room where, before me, a man lived and died, and whose belongings it took me the better part of a year to clear away. When I finally made the room mine, I took a block of charcoal soap and traced the edges of it, the way I once traced salt around the perimeter of my mother's patio. Folk magic, maybe. Hope, certainly.

During the day I work the lot at a car dealership: moving an Ioniq 5 off the gravel, walking back to the main lot, dropping the keys in the used-car manager's box, finding the next farm-hand task. Eighteen dollars an hour. There are "opportunities to advance," they tell me. I would rather be a maverick — a trucker with projects on the side. An uncle once told me nobody likes a maverick. As a boy I half-believed our family name was a mispronunciation of the word.

I have been housed since June of 2025 and employed since April of that year. I stopped the psychiatric injections in December 2025. I quit the "wisdom weed" earlier this year, and the alcohol too — not because they were a problem, exactly, but because I decided they don't add to my life. I am still working on the cigarettes.

When I dictate these memories, I often do it out loud, walking or riding my bike, and I have a habit of stopping myself mid-sentence to say: *I'm here now. I continue. I exist.* It is how I come back when the past pulls too hard. You will see it throughout this book. It is the closest thing I have to a prayer.

This is the story of how I got here — from a boy who learned to live in a private sky, through a decade of falling, to a man trying, for the first time, to stand on the ground.

## Before Memory — Origins (1989–1997)

I was born on September 2, 1989, in Portland, Oregon.

My parents met in Utah. My father was attending BYU — he never graduated — and my mother, a Chilean immigrant, had moved from Rexburg to Provo. They conceived me there and then moved to Oregon, where my mother's sister had already settled. My mother's father had brought the family into the Mormon church in the 1960s; my father's side was Mormon through and through, with a strain of folk magic running down from my paternal grandmother — crystals, trinkets, a sense that signs and tokens carried real power. My mother was raised in the faith but held it loosely. My father held it like a life raft.

My father grew up in Minnesota and Nebraska. He is reserved, prone to silence and stonewalling. My mother is innocent in a way that is almost childlike; she used to joke that she was "an alien from another country." I have an older brother, Quinton. Later, when I was six, a younger brother, Nick, was born.

I have one memory from that early time that nobody will confirm. I see my parents fighting, and my mother jumping from a ledge and breaking her leg. Both of them have told me I wasn't there. I remember it vividly anyway. I have come to believe that something of that conflict — my father's hand, my mother's fear — went into my body before I had words for it, and stayed there as a stored-up charge of fight-or-flight that I have carried my whole life.

When I was three or four, my mother met the man who would become my stepfather, Rich — a former high-level hockey goalie who narrowly missed the 1980 Olympics because of an injury, and who later built a successful career as a home-building contractor around Portland. We lived at First Place in Hillsboro. The house was remodeled while we lived there, and I graduated from sharing a room with Quinton to having one of my own. My cousin Matt was my closest friend in those days. We are no longer in touch.

I was a frightened sleeper. Nightmares came often, and bedtime filled me with dread; I learned to fake sleep in the car so I could be carried in and skip the routine altogether. Rich put me in soccer early and coached my teams himself — the Fire Ants, the Purple People Leaders, later Team Ice. I played for nearly a decade. I never chose it, and I never knew why I was doing it. I was already learning to please.

At age five, at a neighbor's house, I had a sexual experience with an older child. I buried it so deep that for years I told even myself a different story about my "first time." But it shaped everything that came after: a shame I couldn't name, a sense that I was already too much and already spoiled before I had reached any age of accountability. I believe this is where I first left my body and moved upstairs, into what I would later call **Sky City** — a private, floating place where nothing could touch me. To this day, Legos can make my chest tighten, because that is what we played with at that house.

## I. The First Knowing (Ages 9–12)

*1998–2001*

### Browns Drive

Around 1998, we moved from First Place to Browns Drive, out in rural Hillsboro off Laurel Road and Bald Peak. I finished third grade with my mother shuttling Quinton and me back to our old school, Peter Boscow Elementary. In fourth grade I started at Farmington View.

The new house had an elevator — an old, noisy thing with a lever to go down and a single button you had to hold to go up. I loved it. In my head it worked like the Great Glass Elevator in *Charlie and the Chocolate Factory*: when you went down, you were really going up. Downstairs was a pool table, a big old AM/FM stereo, and a door Rich had put in to make himself an office. He didn't need an office. It was a figurative space where he went to plot out his competitive edge. Lars Larson was always on the radio.

There was a drafting table down there too, and one of the truest memories I have of being fathered is this: Rich sitting with me and showing me how to tape a drawing to a drafting board — one corner, then the opposite corner, then the other two, smoothing it flush with your hand. He took the time. He kept the fire going.

My room on Browns Drive was an add-on, with fake dark wood paneling that didn't match the rest of the house. It got infested with ants — hundreds of them. Nick, my little brother, didn't even get a room; he slept in the hallway. Nick and I shared a green-paneled bathroom.

### School and glasses

I got glasses in Ms. McDowell's third-grade class. She yelled at me for "playing around" when I pulled them out of my pocket during reading. I hated them. Nobody else in my family wore glasses — why me? I would wear them, then contacts, for the next twenty-five years.

In fourth grade I was in Mr. Merrill's class with Mike Morrison, Nicole Burgess, and Kip, an autistic boy. Mr. Merrill read dragon stories aloud and splashed water on our arms and heads for effect, which I thought was wonderful. I drew an elk in his class that my parents still say is the best drawing ever. I also caught myself mimicking one of his nervous tics, and I made a decision that would govern the next twenty years of my life:

*Just become a social automaton. Blink when they say blink. Jump when they say jump. Nothing bad can happen. Mimicry is the ticket. Just copy and please people.*

Nobody understood me, and nobody ever challenged me. I was a Jekyll-and-Hyde kid. I never had a trustworthy adult. I never talked to anybody, never opened up, never got curious — but I had already drawn a lot of conclusions, and I thought I needed to be cool.

### Things that seemed like seams

I liked action figures and *Beast Wars*. I'd finished all of Roald Dahl and moved on to survival books — *Hatchet*, *Brian's Winter*. I don't know why. That's just what I was into.

Things seemed — *seamed* — a lot, in those years. As if time and space were a fabric and I could see the stitching. *Obviously this is a fake world*, I would think.

We went to the video store constantly. Movies were practically sacred in our house. We had a giant big-screen TV that Rich loved to brag about, and a cabinet full of VHS tapes — Disney, action, *Total Recall*. I could spend ten minutes just looking at the spines and daydreaming. Browsing the video store was a dream and a free ride to somewhere else, and somewhere else was better than here.

We visited family friends, Lisa and Guy Belt, who talked grown-up things like analog versus digital cell phone plans. I tuned out and drifted off to their computer room. I don't know where I went when I tuned out. But I went there.

When my parents played indoor soccer at a place called Indoor Goals, I would walk in loops around the arcade, pretending to play the games, hoping that if I circled enough I'd forget how bad I felt and wake up somewhere else. Hyper-isolation disguised as freedom.

I habitually opened the junk drawer, as if one day I'd find treasure in there that would carry me to Mount Zion.

### Two fathers

On weekends I visited my father, John. He forced us to celebrate Christmas Eve at his apartment. He said he loved us and was a good dad, but he made me feel icky and unsafe — bad vibes. He drilled into me, weekend after weekend, that he had ADHD, a "chemical imbalance," and that this was why his life hadn't worked out. It wasn't his fault. I absorbed that lesson deeply: that you can locate the source of your failures outside yourself. It would take me decades to unlearn it.

Then there was the policing. My father began watching for signs of masturbation, making comments about "morning wood." I discovered it on my own, in the shower in that green bathroom, at eleven, and I let everyone — including myself — believe that was my first sexual experience. I played along with my father like a devil's advocate and privately thought him a fool.

### Friends, money, and confusion

Josh Miller came over to play. I wanted to impress him with *Time Stalkers* on the Dreamcast, and worried the whole time that he'd notice we were poorer than his family, who lived way up Bald Peak and had a trash compactor. At lunch one day, a kid named Rafa asked me, "Do you spit or swallow?" I had no idea what it meant and felt hot, powerless fury. Everything was topsy-turvy and everyone acted like it was fine. I wanted answers. The answers never came — just more fantastical Hollywood stardom.

I went to church to fit in. I was born into it. Someday, I told myself, I'd quit soccer and use church as the excuse.

### September 11, 2001

I was in Ms. Hoffmeister's sixth-grade class. That morning Rich was driving me to school; I don't remember where my brothers were. I remember standing in my parents' room watching the broadcast. It wasn't scary. It was loud in a silent, supernatural way.

## II. The Storm Years (Ages 13–19)

*2002–2008*

### The Citadel

If I had to name the shelter I built in these years, I'd call it **The Untouchable Bubble** — a dissociative armor, a citadel of my own jurisdiction. Inside it, nothing landed. I had no bullies. I met no social friction. Whatever people said or did bounced right off me, and because I looked so unbothered, people assumed I knew what I was doing. They followed me. I was leading them off cliffs.

I didn't go through the emotional turmoil of puberty because I wasn't really present for it. I barely even grew. I've come to believe that this is exactly why, at thirty-seven, I am living the storm years now — doing at this age the work I should have done at fifteen. Some days it feels like Ra Paulette, the man who carves enormous sandstone caves in New Mexico — except I'm sealed inside Aladdin's cave with too much sand and too little vision, and sometimes I just sit here, blue and purple.

### Middle school

In 2002 I started seventh grade at J.B. Thomas Middle School. A math teacher, Mr. Milam, said, "Stupid people just sit there. Smart people ask questions." A teacher named Ms. Wisely encouraged me to keep challenging the status quo. I got contacts and spent half an hour every morning in the bathroom fixing my hair — a deep-seated pain coming through as vanity.

I lived a double life from the start. At church I hung around Spencer and Chad, acquaintances more than friends. At school and in the band I was someone else.

The band was everything. My friend Will Koehnke, whom I'd met at Farmington View, his little brother Kevin on drums, my friend Nick, and for a while Rob Harlan from the soccer team. Our first show was in a barn when I was fourteen or fifteen — a crowd, people saying we sounded like The Doors. We won our school's first Battle of the Bands, and lost the second to a band called Couch, which we considered a grave injustice. Every Monday for four years I went to Will's house to practice. My bandmates were getting high; I didn't smoke, but I was tripping all the same, on the stored-up charge in my body.

Will saw through me more than anyone. In seventh grade we flipped through his mother's book of illustrated sex positions and came to a picture of a man masturbating. We both laughed. His laugh was genuine; mine was macabre, hiding something. He never forgot it. He wrote a song about me: **"You may be a robot, but I'm not a toy."** I played it dozens of times and never once asked him what he meant. He also wrote a song called **"The Storm"**: *There's a storm in the distance — where you gonna go?* I didn't listen. These were the years when it was socially acceptable to ask for help. I never did.

### The year I quit

Around thirteen, I quit soccer. It asked too much of me — it asked me to be in my body, and my body was where the shame lived. I started going to church regularly with my father, which no one required of me. Quinton didn't go.

In eighth grade I broke my elementary mold. I listened to Sid Vicious and the Sex Pistols. My friends were smoking and drinking their parents' liquor; I tried smoking oregano rolled in paper until my mother caught me. I went to school drunk on half a bottle of Listerine and threw up in the middle of a teacher's classroom. I passed out in the back of classes without anyone noticing and fell down the stairs. I wanted to die and be reborn, though not in those words. I wanted the hell to change. A girl named Karina Hoffman liked me, everyone told me so, and I shunned her — I think because I secretly hated myself.

There was a wart, or what seemed like a wart, on my left hand. Only Nick knows this: I heated metal rods and tried to burn it off, thinking *kill the alien within me.*

Then I did the unthinkable. I turned into the perfect Mormon boy.

### The good kid

By freshman year I'd quit drinking. I adopted the good-Mormon-guy persona wholesale — a peter-priesthood, early-morning-seminary, scripture-memorizing zealot. Around fourteen and three-quarters, I latched onto Mormonism unconsciously, as a safety mechanism. Church leaders were impressed and fed the zeal, and since I didn't have to choose anything, I was all in. It felt like a free ride. I blazed up trails and flew down mountains on my snowboard with boundless energy — because I wasn't in the driver's seat. Someone else was.

For about two years, starting around thirteen or fourteen, I busked at the Hillsboro Saturday farmers market in a brown felt hat with my father's old Silvertone acoustic. I was terrified, but I did it. Some Saturdays I made over a hundred dollars:

> *The Times They Are a-Changin'* · *Blowin' in the Wind* · *Folsom Prison Blues* · *Ring of Fire* · *He Was a Friend of Mine* · *Yonder Comes a Sucker* · *You Are My Sunshine* · *Mr. Tambourine Man*

My father built his identity around my success in the church — the seeds he planted had taken root. I'd go to his house on weekends, go on a Scout campout, come back for church, and drive home to my mom's without really talking to anyone. Communication with my parents was terrible. They bit their tongues and trusted that I was fine, because on the surface I was.

### Scenes from the storm

- **Brent Wong's birthday, seventh grade.** They put me in a trash can and ding-dong-ditched me on a stranger's porch. I got yelled at and went home with my tail between my legs. Brent teased me about it for years.
- **Minnesota, age fourteen.** A trip with my father and Quinton to see my uncle Dave and his new family. I don't remember feeling bad, which means I slammed the door on it; I was always miserable with the two of them.
- **The shirt.** I stole a shirt from my grandma Darlene's house — a cartoon animal with braces, a size too small. I never owned up to it.
- **Las Vegas.** Visiting my grandma Roseanne, I met a country musician named Rich with an eagle tattoo on his shoulder, and came home pushing country music on the band.
- **Grandma moves in.** My grandmother came to live with us in my old room, alone, on oxygen, missing her husband, while I moved into Quinton's room and shut myself in with my guitar. I hyper-isolated. I wish I'd sat with her. I still feel that.
- **The collarbone.** I provoked Quinton until he threw me to the ground and broke my collarbone. I set that kind of trap my whole life: provoke, turn the other cheek, prove you're the violent one. He was the only person in that house who would really mess with me — who tried to draw me out of my bubble.
- **The Honda.** At sixteen I fixed up a 1988 Honda Civic, swapping in the transmission from a wrecked one. I got a job at Taco Time to pay for gas. Will got a job there too. People followed me because I looked like I knew what I was doing.
- **Moses on the intercom.** Sophomore or junior year I put on a gray beard, grabbed a big stick and the lunchroom microphone, and delivered a satirical doomsday sermon. I got applause. I did cringe things all through those years and was always applauded, and it taught me the wrong lesson: that you could mess around forever and everything would work out. I carried that until 2020.
- **Guys and Dolls.** Junior year I sang in the school musical. I secretly hated it.
- **Kangaroo.** Junior year I played "Tie Me Kangaroo Down, Sport" at the year-end festival and it was a hit. Senior year I tried the same trick, unplugged, and bombed.
- **Sadie and Courtney.** I made out with Sadie Lopez once or twice; at her house she wanted more, and my body simply didn't respond. I left. Her friend Courtney gave me a huge hickey that I wore like a badge of honor. I was more messed up than a thrown-away half-baked potato.

I barely ate lunch in high school. I floated table to table picking up conversations, a Cartoon Network song stuck in my head — *When I eat beans, I sit in my own little cloud; nobody comes to visit me in my little cloud.* I lived in that cloud for years. Looking back, it was a huge deal. Eating with others is how we tether to each other.

I had one dream I wrote down on a typewriter: I fell into a giant pit. There was a rope to climb out, but before I was smart enough to use it, I forgot about it, and the rope went away. Sometimes I feel I'm still in that dream.

### Grades, Eagle, and the exit

Freshman year, straight A's. Sophomore year, two B's. Junior year I took advanced classes and got B's and C's. Senior year I took the easiest classes and skimmed by — shop, CAD drafting Lego blocks in Autodesk Inventor.

I got my Eagle Scout right at eighteen. The project was the easiest thing I could think of: I called Parks and Rec, posted a notice at church, and twenty or thirty people cleared weeds out of a city park. At the banquet I put my face over Bill Clinton's in a framed presidential photo. Underneath it all I was a baby crying for nutrients.

One thing from these years I truly cherish: every Mother's Day, Rich took us all out for brunch.

I graduated in 2008 and went to the all-night drug-free party. I didn't go to college that first year. I recorded an album of songs about how sincere and devoted I was — *let me show you the painting of my crystal mountain view* — and dreamed of selling it at the BYU bookstore. I applied to BYU and was accepted, and I put in my papers for a mission. It was the easiest thing. I didn't have to choose anything. Nobody stopped to ask, *Justin, are you actually here?*

## III. The Open Field (Ages 18–29)

*2007–2018*

This chapter was supposed to be my launching. Instead it was the fall from Sky City — the slow disintegration of everything that had kept me floating.

### Prelude: 2007

At eighteen I watched Aaron Russo's *America: Freedom to Fascism* and got scared about the modern world. When Hillary Clinton came to town, my friend Nick Giannopoulos and I were the only protesters there, holding a 9/11 truth sign. My parents always said I was a free spirit. Even then, I valued freedom and autonomy above almost anything. I didn't notice that I was also plugged straight into the machinery I distrusted.

### The mission: Sonora, Mexico (2008–2010)

I was so proud that I already knew Spanish and only needed three weeks at the Missionary Training Center. In the MTC my companion was Elder Anaya. I arrived in Mexico three weeks ahead of my cohort.

The mission president took me to a VIP restaurant. When the waitress dropped the menus, I just stood there, in my own world. I think that was his test, and I failed it without knowing.

I served first in Hermosillo, in a neighborhood called Jardines — mostly poor, subsidized housing — in a trio with Elders Pulido and Morton. Morton was like my brother Quinton; we stonewalled each other for six weeks. Then Pulido alone, then Matías. Our mission president changed from Omar Villalobos, a salesman, to Sergio Vélez, an educator; I preferred the salesman then, and I understand why now. I became a trainer. With my trainee, Elder Niquet, I once lost my temper and screamed *get off my bed!* in English when he sat on it and wouldn't move. He'd found the edge of my fairy-tale goody-two-shoes persona, and the cognitive dissonance sent me spinning. I ignored it, the way I ignored all my feelings.

Then the little border town of Sonoita with Elder Cruz, whom everyone picked on for being different; then Elder Murillo in Obregón; and finally Guaymas, a beach town, for my last ten months, with companions including Clark and Galicia. By then I had checked out mentally. A sister missionary wrote the lyrics of the Beatles' "Two of Us" in my little book when I left.

I baptized people: **Jesús Tenorio**, a trucker who hauled dynamite, and his family; a mother and daughter both named Patricia, and the daughter's sister; a man called Chano in Obregón; **Felipe Bello**, a stonemason, and his family in Sonoita — in a leaking font, so they were baptized sitting down; a family who had fled the violence of Ciudad Juárez; a boy named Luis; two brothers, Oswaldo and Enrique, whose mother was overjoyed. I hate that I sold them what I now see as a false product of eternal families. I was a shining light on a hill, I told myself. The light was artificial. My feet and body took a beating, and I masked poverty anxiety and hatred as calm and wisdom the whole time.

### BYU (2011–2015)

In January 2011 my mother, my father, and Rich all came to Provo to set me up in my off-campus housing at Liberty Square, where my mission friend John Talent lived. It was real support, and I'm grateful for it.

I didn't know how to work. I fell asleep in lectures, got B's and C's, and did fine only in Spanish. I worked at the MTC as a Spanish teacher among zealots. I took Latin because my patriarchal blessing mentioned "governmental and physical laws" and I thought that meant I should become a lawyer. I failed it in truth if not on paper. I invited a girl named Marcy over for Valentine's Day, failed to woo her, and told no one. I made out with Ali Clark and Abby Cruz and drove away from those relationships as fast as I'd started them. I went Latin dancing at a place called Pirates in Orem. I took CS classes with my friend Hector Jiménez. I lived in a house called Lone Tree with John and a pair of Oregon guys, and I followed John into longboarding and into his upper-echelon Mormon worldview, terrified of him and masking it as calm.

I lived one summer with **Kevin Fudge**, an Army ROTC leader who was Mormon but not *Mormon* — who had true ground. If I'd been wiser I would have stayed close to him. He's one of the people I most regret losing touch with.

In the summer of 2012 I ran the Deseret News Marathon in about three hours and thirty-three minutes. That summer, too, I met **Nicté-Ha Martínez** — beautiful, intelligent. She made me eggs just because, we went running together, we kissed on her doorstep. I sometimes say I should have married her, then catch myself repeating my father, who always said he should have married my mother's college roommate.

### Bolivia (Winter 2012)

My Spanish classmate Matt Moen invited me on a BYU internship abroad, and I spent the winter term of 2012 in Bolivia teaching employment workshops. It remains one of the core highlights of my life, because I am of the Andes on my mother's side, and down there I felt connected to my blood.

We lived on the eighth floor of a building in La Paz next to the church, beside a family — Lawrence and Heidi Chávez and their three kids — who treated me with such warmth that Heidi reminded me of my own Chilean mother. We worked with the employment center's director, Enrique Cabezas, and his assistant Víctor Agramonte, driven around by Eduardo Borgoa. We traveled to five of Bolivia's nine departments: **La Paz, Potosí, Oruro, Tarija, and Sucre.** We saw the San Francisco monastery, the Casa de la Moneda where Spanish silver was minted and shipped around the world, the vineyards of Tarija, the dual capital of Sucre, and the Valle de la Luna. We played soccer every week on the church court. During Carnival someone pelted me with eggs. In Oruro I danced merengue with a girl whose name I've lost and almost stayed the night — and didn't, because I was culturally Mormon. A dermatologist in La Paz tried to laser away the redness in my face. I see now that the redness was shame.

Matt and I flew north to Cobija, in Pando, where my mother's sister lives, and spent Carnival week with my cousins Luna, Simón, and Valentina. My aunt drove us through the Brazilian state of Acre and made sure we knew who Chico Mendes was. We ate wild boar and other exotic meats and swam in a lake with Brazilian friends. It was a part of my life I never knew existed.

### Marriage (2013–2014)

Back in Provo I lived at the Muffin Top house with a musician named Ian Ray. At church I recognized a girl I'd met at a dance back in Portland. I knocked on her door. She answered, *Hey, bud.* That was **Olivia**. I kissed her on her parents' doorstep at Thanksgiving 2012, and in June 2013 we were married in the temple — my parents, the ones who actually raised me, waiting outside because they couldn't attend the ceremony. The temple words moved me to tears. We honeymooned at Disneyland and posed under the "happily ever after" mural.

I wasn't prepared to face myself. I had believed that marriage and sex would make me happy, and when they didn't, I fell apart. I worked at BYU's Office of IT, then at a lab under Brett Stone, supposedly building a team-optimization tool and producing nothing in eight months; then freelance web work on a professor's Rialto website, where I produced nothing again. I started smoking marijuana — at school, at home, until I passed out on the couch — and I drew Olivia into drinking and smoking with me. I lied to her about being high and enjoyed getting away with it. In a men's meeting at church, a man stood up and said *stay the course, don't deviate,* and it landed on me like a prophecy of what would happen if I didn't.

I stonewalled. I arranged stuffed animals on the table in passive-aggressive tableaux. In October 2014, on our second trip to Disneyland, dressed as Thor for Halloween, I hit the eject button. I told her I wanted a divorce, in sweet, manipulative language: *It's not you, it's me. You'll always be my angel.* That November we divorced. Months later she asked if she was still my angel, and I told her I'd made it up to please her. In the summer of 2015 I sent her a message so out of bounds that she told me never to contact her again. I tried once more years later from a secret email and never heard back. She has since remarried. Once, she saw my parents at her job, and they were kind to each other, because my parents are good people.

I am not proud of how I treated her. I took her hostage to my unraveling. I think she eventually saw the religion as the deeper culprit, and I think she may be out of it too.

### Falling (2015)

My last year at BYU I was a full-fledged pothead and, in my own mind, a proud Satanist — smoking in campus bathrooms, then falling asleep at a desk to Binary Star. I worked on the grounds crew mowing lawns and planting flowers. My roommates came and went: Jesse, Derek Addison, Muhammad and his cousin. I took a tab of acid with Marcos and Derek and tripped all night until, at dawn, I found a stillness beside the Provo River — and Marcos snapped me out of it. I started smoking a tobacco pipe by that river. My history capstone, on Native American history, was never completed. They gave me a C.

That winter I checked stacks of books out of the BYU library and never read them; I was looking for an exit, like Macaulay Culkin in *The Pagemaster*. But two of those books changed my life.

The first was a Native American prayer book that said, in essence: *When you are afraid, look to one star, even if it's only one, and let it orient you.* That star got me through the next chapter.

The second was a pamphlet I found in the religion section: *The Truth About the Rastafarians* by G.K. Osei. It asked one question in simple language and answered it with one idea. It told me I didn't have to throw out the baby with the bathwater. It introduced me to Haile Selassie, and to a framework for being human after Mormonism. I have been, in my way, a clean-faced Rasta ever since.

In June 2015 my parents drove to Utah in the gold Mercedes, put a bike rack on it, and brought me home to Oregon. I kept up appearances the whole drive. Rich was starting to notice.

### New York (October 2015)

I bought a one-way ticket to an entrepreneurship conference in New York, telling myself I'd hitchhike home. I sat in the sessions with headphones on, believing the music gave me some kind of power. Afterward I lived in JFK airport for three or four days, posted a GoFundMe that no one answered, and finally walked out onto the highway, on the wrong side. Paramedics came. When they asked if I wanted to talk to someone about mental health, I thought they meant a counselor. They meant the beginning of a decade.

I was taken to Long Island Jewish's youth ward — I was twenty-five but looked younger. I treated it like another fantastical trip. A nurse sang Bob Marley's "Black Survivors" with me. I told staff about what happened when I was five, and they validated me — and, without ever saying so to my face, wrote a diagnosis into my record. That began what I think of as ten years of psychiatric oversight, from 2015 until December 2025. Rich bought me a ticket home.

### The blur (2016–2017)

Back at my parents' house, I sat in my room and smoked and imagined I would build an empire. I stole money from my brother. I substitute taught and contributed nothing. Rich and I got physical once — he threw me against a wall while I came at him — and he was patient for a long time until he couldn't be. In 2016, wanting attention more than death, having read about the "play of the mind" in Dennis Waite's *The Book of One*, I hung myself from a thin rope knowing Rich was close enough to hear. He pulled me down. Another time I drank a bottle of NyQuil and threw it up. In a psych ward that year I met a woman; after we got out we slept together twice in a hotel. I was at the height of madness, looking for a trip, not for healing.

2017 I barely remember. I was probably hospitalized a couple of times. I was making my parents' lives a living hell.

### 2018: Provo, Nebraska, Hood River

In January 2018 I moved back to Provo and worked at McDonald's and at Cougar Pizza, where my manager, Joe, caught me in a lie and reported me to my sober-living house for smoking hash and getting drunk at the shop after hours. I spent that summer with my aunt in Nebraska, then lived with my father, who had married in 2013 or 2014 a woman named Michelle with three children. When his life collapsed — he was a hoarder, and my mother helped him clear his house — he loaded a moving truck to go back to his parents in Nebraska. I rode with him as far as Hood River, cursed him, got out, slammed the door, and flipped him off as he drove away "like a sad little rabbit."

He would later be convicted of charges involving a minor — his stepdaughter — and incarcerated, likely for the rest of his life. I say no comment on the case itself. I'll only say that he put on the clothes of a father but failed, when it mattered, to protect his son and teach him how to deal with the real world.

I stayed in Hood River, homeless, sleeping on a blanket in a gravel pit. I made friends with other unhoused men — Randall, Chase, Jay, an old man I think was a vet whose name I've lost — and two skater kids, Elias and Riker, who would come by to check on me. A businessman named Aaron tried to assess my situation and help; I told him to get lost. I panhandled for weed. I dumped out bottled water from Safeway's doorstep to return the bottles for joint money. It is one of the most shameful chapters of my life.

Around Halloween 2018 I tried to steal eight dollars' worth of things from Walmart, a Halloween mask among them. I spent about ten days in county jail and took a plea deal. When they learned I had a diagnosis they offered to move me to the psych unit, and I said yes, because it was the path of least resistance. When they released me I went straight back to the gravel pit, found a joint, and lit it.

Back in the Portland area I moved in with a man I knew as Douglas — I later learned his name was Will Mullins. That November or December, I used an online service to formally resign my name from the church's records.

There's one more thing I have to say about that time, because it explains so much of my falling. Between the temple's covenants — *if you fail to live up to every covenant made at these altars, you will be in my power* — and the old belief in a mirror world where your dreams and agency are stolen, I came to truly believe I was in Satan's power. I thought I had no other option, so I threw my life away by degrees. One night at Douglas's I looked in a mirror and saw myself cross-eyed, a person I had never seen before, and it terrified me.

In September 2018 I turned twenty-nine. The launching chapter ended. I hadn't launched. I'd been lying to myself about lying to myself.

## IV. The True Reckoning (Ages 30–45)

*2019–present*

This is the chapter I am living. I am halfway through it. Its name fits better than any other: this is where I met myself face to face.

### 2019: The lot and the paintings

From January to November 2019 I worked as a lot guy at Royal Moore, a certified pre-owned dealership, and I was housed. I offset my insecurity by buying random things at Goodwill and filling a storage unit with my "life treasure." I lived with Douglas again for a while; then in Aloha with a young homeowner named Yarrow and her boyfriend Gregor, until she told me to take my own road; then with a man named Mario, whose father was from Sonora, until friction and my isolation — and my pot smoking — ended that too. At Mario's I nailed canvases to a tree in the rain, a stone's throw from civilization, and painted feng shui paintings as if everything were fine, with supplies from the New Renaissance bookstore.

In 2019 I also found a spiritual home in the teachings of **Mooji** and Advaita, which I'd first touched in 2015 through Dennis Waite's *The Book of One*. It gave me a question I still use: not *Who is God?* — which loops forever — but *Who am I?* I am not the mind with its infinite questions, nor the body that withers. Whatever remains — pure being — I am that. *There is nothing to fix. There is nothing wrong.*

### 2020: The walk

In early 2020 I spent two weeks in Las Vegas carrying a walking stick across my back like a cross, walking up and down the city every day without a word to say. I had started taking the words of Christ literally — not because I believed, but because it was ironic that I finally had the chance to take up my cross and walk. I hauled my Goodwill treasure to Las Vegas and Arizona and eventually let it fall by the wayside. That walk freed something. I don't buy random things anymore.

In January 2020 I moved to Arizona to be near my grandmother. I was homeless for a month and ate out of garbage cans. I reached out to a mental-health organization whose owner, by coincidence, shared my name: **Justin David Lee Harvey** of Just One Life. He helped stabilize me for two or three months as COVID arrived. I worked as a janitor at Barnes & Noble. I lived in a backyard RV on a property owned by Kevin Crosby and his girlfriend Leslie, until I made unauthorized changes to the space and gave it up. I tried to walk, then bike, to Mormon Lake. I failed both times. I came back to Oregon, tried living with Douglas once more, and ended up on the street.

Then, in the spring of 2020, I walked.

I walked from Hillsboro to Tillamook on about fourteen dollars of food over four days, one of them a fast. Then south down the coast — Newport, Florence — for roughly three months, aiming for Los Angeles. Food stamps and walking eighteen hours a day. A young woman named Shiloh gave me a ride near Honeyman State Park. In every new town I could feel people tense up — a stranger walking in without money. I started cutting my blanket down to scraps because when you walk that far you just fall to the ground and sleep in whatever you have.

In Fort Bragg, sleeping in a teepee, I heard a roar like a wild animal and lit my lighter to keep the fear away. A PhD student named **Tim Epps** gave me a fish sweater I slept curled inside, knees pulled up into the torso, and drove me to Trinidad, California — where I heard the roar again and hid beneath a bench. I heard it a third time, much later, from my parents' shed. I don't know what it was. I know it happened.

I made it to Marin County — San Rafael, Corte Madera. I met a man with no arms. I tried to find the Golden Gate Bridge and couldn't. Whatever mental math had been pushing me forward under the stars ran out, and I turned back. Near Brookings I collapsed on the side of the road. In the hospital, Dr. Flowers didn't call me mad. She said I was exhausted, overworked. When I left Brookings, Steven Holiday gave me a blanket. In Coos Bay my mother bought me a bus ticket to Eugene and on home.

In December 2020 I went back to Arizona.

### 2021: Arizona, Colorado, the wheelbarrow

I stayed at the East Valley Men's Center in Mesa and worked at McDonald's again, then moved into a house of older men — Leroy, Jim, Pascual — and a gravedigger named Viper who moved into my room. In May or June I quit and walked the streets again.

An acquaintance from my mission, **Enrique Gómez**, invited me to live in his tiny trailer home in Steamboat Springs, Colorado. I worked at Brooklyn's Pizza and trained in jiu-jitsu. Things deteriorated over sobriety, and I was hospitalized in Colorado — Denver was frightening. As the weather turned cold, around September, I left for Utah.

In Springville — where my uncle lives, though we didn't have the relationship to bridge the gap — I bought a wheelbarrow with my last pizzeria paycheck, painted it blue, chalked words on it, set a cinder block inside in honor of a real long-distance wheelbarrow walker, and started pushing it toward Tooele. I bought landscaping tools too; I had a dream of an underground landscaping service. Somewhere along the way I approached a man at a closed bank, and he called the police. At a 7-Eleven I had to come out and answer their questions. I learned something there that I've never forgotten: if your clothes are good, you're worthy; if your vehicle is a wheelbarrow, you must have stolen it. A stranger gave me one ride on the highway.

In Tooele I stayed at a transient house with four or five other men. They took my wheelbarrow, which broke my heart a little. Two social workers, **Christy and Nadine**, stayed in touch and bought me a bus ticket home to Oregon.

I moved in with my mother and Rich on Shadow Lane. In October 2021 I went to a cousin's wedding.

### 2022–2023: Floors, kitchens, and the hut

In February 2022 I was sleeping on my mother's floor and working at McDonald's. That spring I worked at the Old Spaghetti Factory, holding it down for a while, until I fell back into self-soothing with a weed vape and took my parents' car to the smoke shop. Rich asked me straight out why I'd taken it, and I couldn't answer. He called me an asshole. It was true. Not that I wanted to be — but that's what I was.

From September 2022 to January 2023 I washed dishes and helped with harvests at a butcher shop in Hillsboro.

In April 2023 I had LASIK surgery at NVision in Beaverton. After twenty-five years of glasses and contacts, I could see. It felt like a marker of starting over.

Not long after, frustrated and not liking myself or my life, I threw a backpack near my mother in her house, and she told me I wasn't welcome anymore. A new sub-chapter began, wandering Hillsboro. An old friend, **Madison Wellington**, whom I ran into at the farmers market, gave me a hammock and a key to his workshop; I ate his whole stockpile of Girl Scout cookies out of loneliness, and I still feel bad about it. With his hatchet and saw I cleaned up a fallen tree by the McDonald's off Cornell and hauled the branches away on ropes in a berserker, mad-monk state — and a local contractor, **Adrian Roman**, saw me and put me to work digging and painting alongside his crew, Abel and Arturo. Abel always said *perfectamundo*. Adrian let me sleep on his deck. I drifted away from him, too, unsure why.

For a while I lived in an abandoned hut on public land. One day, like an idiot, I burned blackberry bushes in broad daylight beside a public trail. I was charged with trespassing and arson; the arson charge was dropped. I had to appear in court four or five times, at real cost to me with no transportation. I played the madman with my public defender, and they assigned me a new one. I still have some pipers to pay from that time. I also washed walls for fifty dollars at a property belonging to Andy Karl Soelberg.

Around this time, my father was incarcerated.

### 2023–2024: The backyard

Near the end of 2023 my mother received me back into her world and let me live in her backyard. I salted the perimeter of the patio and washed its edges with charcoal soap. I stayed there about a year.

In February 2024 I set out on another walk, retracing 2020 toward Tillamook, then down the coast. Dimitri gave me a ride; a man named Coyote gave me another. In Florence, at four in the morning, I met a woman I first mistook for a girl. We spent a day together; I kissed her; at a laundromat we were made to feel we couldn't exist there, and I left angry. Her bones felt as hollow as a bird's. At the Roseburg junction, Clinton gave me a ride. In Roseburg I realized I was demolished. Justin David Lee Harvey bought me a ticket home, and a man named **Greg Fort** paid for a hotel that night — it truly saved me.

That spring and summer I barely left the backyard. I used a bucket and snuck it to the dumpster every few days, full of shame. Justin had sent me an iPhone, and I generated art with AI and built a spreadsheet of my life from birth to age fifty-five. I felt entitled to rest after what I'd been through. But I was talking out loud — doing stand-up, essentially, with no one asking — and to my parents it sounded manic. I got the boot a second time.

My parents rented me a storage unit and set it up for me to live in. On the second day I struck up a conversation with the storage attendant; he watched the cameras, knocked on my unit, and told me to get out.

Around Halloween 2024 I talked my way into the garage and woodshop of **Rob Hinton**, my old Scoutmaster, for a couple of months. They had me to dinner once a week, but I grew contemptuous that they didn't invite me inside. One night I said something to his wife — *you're going to have to die* — that she took as a threat. Rob told me to leave. Instead, in primal fear, I cut the lock on the woodshop's attic hatch and hid up there for a couple of days, until I realized it was insane.

My friend **Andy Silver** let me live in his backyard. I stayed there from November 2024 until June 2025.

### The oversight

In October and November 2024, I made what I call a political decision: I accepted psychiatric oversight — a long-acting injectable antipsychotic and about a year of appointments with a clinician named Dr. Richards — through LifeWorks NW, Sequoia Mental Health, and the Hawthorne Walk-In Clinic. Housing and social circumstances were part of why I said yes. I'll be honest here, as this book is meant to be: much of my involvement with mental health over the years was malingering. I saw a way to get attention and care by saying I heard and saw things. I reaped what I'd sown.

In early 2025 I left Andy's for Phoenix, to join a program that places ex-offenders in trucking careers. It didn't work out. Justin David Lee Harvey couldn't rescue me the way I'd hoped. The Phoenix shelter felt like a war zone. At a Pentecostal revival in Mesa they fed me and bought me a sleeping bag — I had been leaning on churches for handouts for a few years by then. A barista at the Mesa library told me to go to the hospital. I did. In Scottsdale they put me on a second antipsychotic on top of the injection.

My grandmother picked me up when I was discharged. It was the last year of her life. That time with her was chill — dinners, walks, real talk. In 2020 I'd been too far gone to know her. This time I did.

Going home, I missed my bus — or thought I did, because a woman at the stop told me it had already come, and then I watched it pass from the Taco Bell. I tore myself apart. Justin bought me another ticket. North of Sacramento, at 2 a.m., snow closed the pass and they talked about turning the bus around. I got off with my one bag and walked up the road, knocking on the doors of stranded trucks — which was ironic, since I'd gone to Arizona to become a trucker. One driver, about twenty-seven, who told me to call him *Capitán*, opened his door. He fed me, let me smoke, played music, and drove me almost to my doorstep — right into the Hillsboro fairgrounds near Andy's house.

### 2025: Ground

That spring I went hard on the job search. In **April 2025** I started at **Dix Hillsboro Hyundai**. I've worked there full-time since, apart from about three months in the summer of 2025.

In **June 2025** I got my own room — the room where a man had died, full of his things. It took me a year to clean it out, top to bottom, and edge its perimeter with charcoal soap.

On **July 20, 2025**, I wrote a **Certificate of Sovereign Being**.

In **December 2025**, I declined to renew the six-month antipsychotic injection, and ended ten years of psychiatric oversight. As I write, I've been off it for about nine months. What the injection did for me, as far as I could tell, was add weight and slow my spirit down; I see no difference in the coherence of my thinking without it. I don't need to be told I've lost contact with reality by a system that has lost contact with the Earth. I know that's my view, and I own it.

### 2026: The Bill of Intention

This year I quit the gummies and the alcohol. I came out to my mother as gay in July, and knew as I said it that I was lying — trying on a box someone else might put me in, because I didn't know which box was mine. I tried the apps. Then I looked closer and realized I'm not gay. People will tell you who you are if you don't know who you are.

I haven't had sex in nine years. I have spent money I didn't have on pornography and paid apps, and earlier this year I fell for a romance scam — someone who claimed to be from Ponca City, Oklahoma, with a Nigerian phone number — and sent Apple gift cards. I came through it. I've been free of pornography for months now. On the road, in 2020, 2021, and 2024, I never needed it. It's when I get comfortable that the parasite of entitlement creeps back in.

I told my mother I was resigning from the dealership to start a walking business. She cried. Rich called with talking points. I stayed, for now, in the precarity.

At work, I'd started making a gesture from the old temple ritual — the first penalty, a line across the throat — as a quiet protest against the violence underneath everything. A coworker thought I was making a death sign at her. My boss came out and led with *you seem stressed*. I denied it. In foolishness I was correct; in wisdom I was wrong. Whatever I meant, what she saw was real to her. Navigation is ongoing.

I joined a Zoom session with a woman who runs Rose City NVC, learning Nonviolent Communication — how to say what I feel and what I need. I've returned to the Saturday meetings of **Falun Dafa**, which I first encountered in 2016: truth, compassion, forbearance — a good three-spoke wheel. I read A.C. Grayling's *The Good Book*, a humanist bible, and a printed allegory of the vineyard. I end my days on forbearance and on Mooji's truth: there's nothing to be done, nothing to fix, nothing wrong.

And this summer I wrote **The Bill of Intention: The Unified Operating Structure** — seven articles, a covenant with myself, designed as a formal document. After a lifetime of bouncing between belief systems — Mormonism and my grandmother's folk magic, Rastafari, Advaita, Pentecostal revivals, Falun Dafa — I concluded that I don't need an external belief system. I need a structure that unifies how I live each day with what I believe, so I don't drown in the fatigue of deciding everything from scratch. My father has lived without a fixed framework for a long time; I shared that, until I branched off.

Alongside it I made a graphical system I call **The Way of the Wheel.** When all six spokes are turning, you get something good:

1. **The Body** — moving, circulating the energy, so the caged animal in me isn't stuck afraid, angry, sad, and disgusted.
2. **Food**
3. **Time**
4. **Home**
5. **Other People**
6. **The one I live with** — myself.

The first spoke, the body, includes making what I call a **luminous map** of the hurts of the past. This book is part of that map.

### Where I stand

I am rough around the edges. I don't think I make people feel at ease, because I'm rarely at ease myself. My body is still braced from the epinephrine it stored up as a toddler while my parents fought. I'm often scared, overconfident, and — I'll say it — sometimes downright deceptive. I'm learning compassionate self-talk in a culture that raised me on beat-'em-up cartoons. It's a seasonal endeavor, not overnight work.

But lately, especially these past few weeks, my mind and body and heart have been working together better than they ever have. I notice when I physically feel unsafe. I'm learning that when I feel unsafe I probably am — and that I can be strong and grounded anyway.

If I don't have a purpose now, I never will. I've defined it.

## V. The Deepening (Ages 45–65)

*Not yet lived. What I've written down for the man I'm walking toward.*

Midlife is not a plateau. It is a vantage point. You can see more from there than you could from the valley floor. You have enough past to pattern-match from and enough future to act into.

There is grief in midlife — for youth, for possibility, for people lost — and it is real and should not be bypassed. This is the decade of turning outward with what you've learned: mentorship, contribution, building something that will outlast you. People who engage in purpose beyond themselves live longer, report more satisfaction, and recover faster from setbacks. This is not sentiment. This is biology.

**What this stage asks of me:**

- Take my health seriously now.
- Pass something on.
- Redefine success.
- Welcome complexity.
- Make peace with unlived lives.

I will arrive here at forty-five, around 2034 — the year my own spreadsheet runs toward. I hope I arrive with a body I've moved, a story I've told honestly, and something to hand to someone younger who is still up in their own Sky City, waiting for someone to ask: *Are you actually here?*

## VI. The Long View (Ages 65+)

*The page is blank. That is the point.*

The folder for this chapter holds almost nothing yet — a placeholder, a promise. I'll reach sixty-five around 2054. I don't know who will be standing there. I know I'd like him to be able to look back over this map and see not a straight road but a life well-walked: Hillsboro to Hermosillo, La Paz to Marin, a wheelbarrow in Utah, a backyard in Oregon, a room of my own. I'd like him to have completed the arc.

- **Kindness**
- **Courage**
- **Presence**

## Epilogue — One Star

In the winter of 2015, in a library at a school I had failed, I read a prayer that said: *When you are afraid, look to one star.*

Five years later I was walking the coast of Oregon and California barefoot or nearly so, with a pair of pants, a shirt, and a scrap of blanket, steering by the stars because I had nothing else. There is nothing like it — coming to grips with your own madness in the middle of the world's madness, and making peace because of the effort you've given.

I was a boy who left his body at five and lived in a cloud. I became a teenager everyone applauded and nobody saw, a missionary selling a painting of a crystal mountain, a husband who ejected, a man who believed he belonged to Satan, a walker, a wheelbarrow pusher, a backyard dweller, a patient, a lot guy, a writer of his own covenant.

I don't need anyone to tell me who I am anymore. I'm still learning — but I'm learning on the ground.

*I'm here now. I continue. I exist.*
