# BECOMING
### An Autobiography, From Birth to Now (1989–2026)

> *I exist. I continue. I'm here now.*

---

## A Note Before Beginning

This is the whole arc so far, put together from the pieces I left in this archive: the chronological record, the dictated memories, and the fragments in each chapter folder. It runs from a delivery room in Portland in 1989 to a rented room in Hillsboro in the autumn of 2026, when I am thirty-seven.

I've tried to tell it the way the chronological record asks: organized by period, not mythologized. Where my memory and other people's memories disagree, I say so. Where I don't remember, I say that too. Some of what follows is hard. I've kept it because leaving it out would turn this into the same pretty painting I spent my youth hanging on the wall.

The chapters follow the Map of Thresholds in the README. I've added a prologue for the years before the First Knowing. The last chapters, *The Deepening* and *The Long View*, haven't been lived yet, so they appear here as what they are: open ground.

## Prologue: Before the First Knowing
*1989–1997 · Ages 0–8*

My parents met in Provo, Utah. My father was a student at BYU who never finished. He grew up in Minnesota and Nebraska and was a quiet, reserved man, given to silence when things got hard. My mother was a Chilean immigrant who had moved down from Rexburg. Her father had brought the family into the Mormon church in the 1960s. I've come to think of it as part faith and part ladder, a way up for a family arriving in a new country.

I was conceived in Utah. By the time I arrived, my parents had followed my mother's sister to Oregon. I was born on **September 2, 1989, in Portland**. The doctor who delivered me was of Indian descent. I had an older brother, Quinton, waiting at home.

My earliest memory is one nobody else will vouch for. I remember my parents fighting, and my mother jumping from a ledge and breaking her leg. Both of them have told me I wasn't there. I remember it anyway, vividly. I don't know what to do with that except keep it. What I'm more sure of is the feeling underneath it: as a very small child I didn't feel safe, and my body learned early to stay braced. I would carry that bracing, that stored-up adrenaline, for the next thirty years.

When I was three or four, my mother met the man who became my stepfather, Rich. He had been a serious hockey goalie who missed the 1980 Olympic team because of an injury, and he later built a successful home-building business around Portland. We lived at First Place in Hillsboro. When the house was remodeled I went from sharing a room with Quinton to having my own. My cousin Matt was my closest friend in those years. We aren't in touch anymore.

I had bad nightmares and a lot of fear around sleep. On the drive home I would pretend to be asleep in the car so I could skip the bedtime routine and be carried in.

Rich put me in soccer early and coached my teams: the Fire Ants, the Purple People Leaders, later Team Ice. It gave me discipline. When the coach says run a lap, you run a lap. It also taught me something less useful: that the way to avoid friction was to do what pleased the adults, whether or not I wanted it.

When I was five, at a neighbor's house, I had a sexual experience with an older child. I've used different words for it over the years. In my plainest moments I call it what it was: I was abused. I buried it so completely that for years I told myself and everyone else a different story about my "first time." I'm convinced it shaped almost everything that came after. It sped me past innocence before I had words, it filled me with shame I couldn't place, and it taught me to hide. The boy had Legos. To this day Legos can set me off.

My younger brother Nick was born when I was six. I went to Peter Boscow Elementary. In third grade, in Ms. McDowell's class, I got glasses. I pulled them out of my pocket during reading one day and she yelled at me for playing around when I put them on. Nobody else in my family wore glasses. *Why me?* I hated them, and I'd wear them or contacts for the next twenty-five years.

## I. The First Knowing
*1998–2001 · Ages 9–12*

Around 1998 we moved out to Browns Drive in rural Hillsboro, off Laurel and Bald Peak. My mom drove Quinton and me back to Peter Boscow so I could finish third grade, and in fourth grade I started at Farmington View.

The new house had an **elevator**: a noisy, old, lever-operated thing. I loved it. In my head it worked like the elevator in *Charlie and the Great Glass Elevator*, where going down somehow felt like going up. Downstairs there was a pool table and a big AM/FM stereo, and a door Rich had put in to make himself an office. He didn't really need an office. It was more of a place to plot. He had a drafting table down there, and one of the clearest good memories I have of him is the day he sat with me and showed me how to tape a sheet down flat: one corner, then the opposite one, then the last two, smoothing it with your hand. Lars Larson was always on the radio.

My room was an add-on with fake dark wood paneling that didn't match the rest of the house. One year it was overrun by hundreds of ants. Nick, still small, didn't get a real room at all and slept in a hallway space. Nick and I shared a green-paneled bathroom.

**Fourth grade** was Mr. Merrill's class, with Mike Morrison, Nicole Burgess, and a boy named Kip. Mr. Merrill read dragon stories out loud and acted them out, and I thought he was creative. I drew an elk that my parents called the best drawing ever. I also copied Mr. Merrill's unconscious tic and hoped nobody would notice. That was my strategy for everything by then. I'd decided, without ever saying it out loud, to become a **social automaton**: blink when they say blink, jump when they say jump, copy and please people, and nothing bad can happen. I had no adult I trusted, never opened up, never asked questions, and had drawn a lot of conclusions anyway. I was a Jekyll-and-Hyde kid, and nobody understood me or challenged me.

I watched *Beast Wars*. I'd finished Roald Dahl and moved on to survival books like *Hatchet* and *Brian's Winter*. I kept opening the junk drawer as if treasure might be waiting in there.

Our family's religion, as much as any church, was **movies**. We went to the video store constantly. We had a big-screen TV that Rich loved to brag about and a cabinet full of VHS tapes: Disney, action movies, *Total Recall*. I could spend ten minutes just staring at the spines, daydreaming. Browsing a video store was a free ride to somewhere other than here.

Even then, the world sometimes felt fake to me, like a fabric where I could see the stitching. *Things seem a lot. Seam a lot.* At the indoor soccer arena, while my parents played, I would walk in loops pretending to play the arcade games, hoping that if I circled long enough I'd wake up somewhere else. At Guy and Lisa Belt's house, while the adults talked about cell phone plans, analog versus digital, I'd drift off to the computer room. I didn't know where I went. I just went.

I visited my father on weekends. He insisted we spend Christmas Eve at his apartment. He said he loved us and that he was a good dad, but being around him felt *icky and unsafe* to me. Over and over he told me that his life had gone badly because of a **chemical imbalance**, his ADHD, and that it wasn't his fault. I absorbed that lesson thoroughly. Years later I'd realize it had given me an escape hatch: if something is broken inside you, nothing is yours to own.

At school there was money anxiety. Josh Miller lived way up Bald Peak in a house with a trash compactor, and when he came over I worried he'd figure out we were poor (we weren't, really) and tried to impress him with *Time Stalkers* on the Dreamcast. A boy named Rafa said something sexual to me in the lunchroom that I didn't understand, and I remember the rage of feeling powerless. Everything was topsy-turvy, everyone acted like it was fine, and I wanted answers that never came.

Around eleven I discovered masturbation in that green bathroom. Not long after, my father started policing it, and I played along. I went to church to fit in. I was already planning, half-consciously, to use church as my excuse to quit soccer someday.

On **September 11, 2001**, I was in Ms. Hoffmeister's sixth-grade class. Rich had driven me to school that morning. I remember watching the coverage in my parents' room. It wasn't scary. It was loud in a silent, supernatural way.

## II. The Storm Years
*2002–2008 · Ages 13–19*

> *There's a storm in the distance — where you gonna go?*
> — Wil Koehnke, "The Storm"

I started seventh grade at **J.B. Thomas Middle School** in 2002. Thirteen and fourteen were my big Boy Scout years. A math teacher, Mr. Milam, liked to say that *stupid people just sit there, smart people ask questions*. Ms. Wisely encouraged me to keep being a strong individual who challenged the status quo. I got contacts and started spending half an hour every morning on my hair.

That was also the year of **the band**. Wil Koehnke and I had met at Farmington View. With Nick (a friend, not my brother), Rob Harlan on drums for a while, and later Wil's little brother Kevin, we played together from about seventh grade until I left on my mission. Our first show was in a barn when I was fourteen or fifteen, and people said we sounded like the Doors. We won our school's first battle of the bands and lost the second year to a band called Couch, which we considered an injustice. For four years I went to practice every Monday. My bandmates got high. I didn't, and I barely registered that they did.

I started living a **double life**: one person at church, another at home and school. I quit soccer around thirteen. Soccer asked me to be present in my body, and being present meant getting close to what had happened when I was five.

In seventh grade, Wil and I paged through his mother's book of sex illustrations and came to a drawing of a man masturbating. We both laughed. His laugh was real. Mine was hiding something. I'm sure he noticed, because for years afterward he'd gently probe at me, and he eventually wrote a song with the line *"You may be a robot, but I'm not a toy."* I played that song dozens of times without once asking him what he meant. He also wrote "The Storm." I never heard the warning in it.

**Eighth grade** was my punk year: long hair, Sid Vicious, the Sex Pistols. Everyone around me was drinking their parents' alcohol and smoking weed. I rolled oregano in paper a few times, until my mother caught me. I drank half a bottle of Listerine before school and threw up in the middle of class. I fell down stairs. Looking back, I wanted to die and be reborn, though I didn't have those words. A girl named Karina Hoffman liked me, everyone knew it, and I pushed her away. I think I already hated myself.

On a trip to see my grandmother Roseanne in Las Vegas, I met a country musician named Rich who had an eagle tattoo on his shoulder. I came home pushing country music on the band. I got an electric guitar and later played harmonica and keyboard too. I always secretly felt like the band's weakest link: my songs were emotional, protest-ish, all right but not great. I never said so.

Then, around fourteen and three-quarters, I flipped. I quit drinking and the half-hearted drug attempts and became a **youthful Mormon zealot**: early-morning seminary, memorized scriptures, the perfect A student, the perfect citizen. Church leaders loved it, and their praise kept the battery charged. I see it now as a safety mechanism I latched onto without choosing. It meant I didn't have to choose anything. Someone else was driving, which is part of why I had so much energy for blazing up trails and bombing down mountains on a snowboard.

I called the place I lived in those years **sky city**, a sealed bubble where nothing touched me. I had no bullies and no real friction. That felt like freedom. I've come to believe it was the thing that crippled me. I never went through the emotional turmoil of adolescence. With nothing to push against, I barely grew.

Some of what I remember from high school:

- **Busking at the Saturday Market** in eighth and ninth grade, in a brown felt hat with an old Silvertone acoustic my father treasured. I played Dylan and Johnny Cash ("The Times They Are a-Changin'," "Blowin' in the Wind," "Folsom Prison Blues," "Ring of Fire," "Mr. Tambourine Man"), sometimes clearing more than a hundred dollars a day. I was scared to do it and did it anyway.
- **Weekends at my father's.** He built his identity on how well I'd taken to the gospel he planted in me. I'd go to his place, go on a Scout campout, come back for church, and drive home to my mom's barely having spoken to her.
- **A 1988 Honda Civic** at sixteen with a broken transmission. I bought a wrecked Civic with a good one and swapped it in. Then I got a job at **Taco Time**, and Wil followed me there, the way people follow someone who looks like he knows where he's going. I didn't.
- **My grandmother** came to live with us, on oxygen and grieving her husband. I moved into Quinton's old room so she could have mine. I shut myself in after school with my guitar and left her alone. That one still hurts.
- **Quinton** was the only person who would mess with me, the only one who tried to pull me out of my bubble. One day I provoked him until he threw me to the ground and broke my collarbone. I held it against him for years. The truth is I had set a trap I set for a lot of people: needle you until you get physical, then turn the other cheek and let you look like the psycho.
- **Theater:** *Guys and Dolls*, junior year. I secretly hated it.
- **The year-end festival:** junior year I played "Tie Me Kangaroo Down, Sport" and it was a hit. Senior year I tried the same song and bombed.
- **The lunchroom:** one day I put on a gray beard, grabbed a big stick and the intercom mic, and delivered a satirical doomsday monologue as Moses. I did a lot of cringe things in those years, and I was applauded for nearly all of them. It taught me you could fool around through life and it would all work out. I believed that until 2020.
- **I mostly didn't eat lunch.** I floated from table to table, picking up conversations, never sitting down to be fed.
- **The wart:** I heated metal rods and tried to burn a wart off my hand, thinking *kill the alien*. Only Nick knows about that.
- **Girls:** I made out with Sadie Lopez. At her house, when she wanted to go further, my body didn't respond and I left. Her friend Courtney gave me a hickey afterward and I wore it like a badge. I had no idea what was good for me.
- **A dream:** I fell into a deep pit with a rope leading out, forgot about the rope, and the rope disappeared. I typed it up on a typewriter. Some days it still feels like the dream I'm in.

My grades went from straight A's freshman year, to two B's sophomore year, to B's and C's in advanced classes junior year, to coasting through basic classes as a senior. I took shop and computer-aided drafting.

There were good things too. Every Mother's Day, Rich took us all out to brunch. I couldn't feel it then, but I appreciate it now.

I made **Eagle Scout** right at eighteen. My project was clearing weeds at Rood Bridge Park with twenty or thirty volunteers I'd recruited with a notice at church. It was so easy it felt like a lie. At the banquet I'd photoshopped my face onto a presidential photo.

In 2007 I watched *America: Freedom to Fascism* and got scared about the modern world. When Hillary Clinton came to town, my friend Nick Giannopoulos and I were the only protesters there, holding a 9/11 Truth sign. My parents always said I was a free spirit. I suppose that was true even inside the machine.

I graduated in **2008**, went to the all-night drug-free party, and didn't go to college. I recorded an album of devotional songs ("This Is His Story," "Crystal Mountain View," "The Storm Out There") and imagined selling it at the BYU bookstore. Listening back, the songs admit what I couldn't: that the faith was, for me, *a pretty painting on the wall*. I was accepted to BYU. I applied for a mission. It was the path of least resistance, and nobody stopped to ask whether I was actually there.

## III. The Open Field
*2008–2018 · Ages 19–29*

### Sonora

I spent only three weeks at the Missionary Training Center because I already spoke Spanish, and I was proud of that. My MTC companion was Elder Anaya. I got to Mexico ahead of my group. On my first day the mission president took me to a VIPS restaurant. When the waitress dropped the menus, I just stood there. I'm sure that told him what he needed to know.

My first area was Jardines in **Hermosillo**: mostly poor, subsidized neighborhoods. I spent the first six weeks in a trio with Elders Pulido and Morton, and it was hell. Morton was like Quinton to me. We were trained to override what our bodies told us for the sake of the mission, and I got very good at it. Then came six weeks with Pulido alone and six with Matías. The mission president changed from Omar Villalobos, a salesman, to Sergio Vélez, an educator. I became a trainer. My companion Niquet sat on my bed one day and wouldn't get off, and I screamed at him in English. He'd found the edge of my fairy-tale character, and I ignored everything that told me.

From there I went to **Sonoita**, a little border town, with Elder Cruz, whom everyone picked on. Then came Murillo, then Obregón, and finally about ten months in the beach town of **Guaymas**, where I was already checking out. A sister missionary wrote the lyrics to the Beatles' "Two of Us" in my notebook when I left.

I baptized people: **Jesús Tenorio**, a trucker who hauled dynamite, and his family. Patricia and her daughters. A man called Chano in Obregón. Felipe Bello, a stonemason in Sonoita, and his family; the font was leaking, so we baptized them sitting down. A family that had fled the violence in Juárez. A boy named Luis Ornelas. Two brothers, Oswaldo and Enrique, whose mother was so happy for them but didn't want baptism herself. Later I would feel I'd sold people a product I didn't believe in. At the time I told myself I was a light on a hill.

I came home in **2010–2011** having masked calm the entire time over poverty anxiety and hatred I didn't know I carried.

### Provo

In **January 2011** my mother, my father, and Rich all drove out together to move me into Liberty Square in Provo. It was a real act of support. I went to class, fell asleep in lectures, made B's and C's, and did fine only in Spanish. I worked at the MTC teaching Spanish to new missionaries, surrounded by people for whom the mission was the peak of their lives.

I lived with John Talent, a mission acquaintance my father had set me up with. He came from an upper-echelon Mormon family and I was afraid of him. I followed him into things like longboarding. Kevin Fudge, my roommate one summer, was a Mormon who wasn't really Mormon, an ROTC leader with real ground under him. He's one of the people I most regret losing touch with. I took Latin because my patriarchal blessing mentioned governmental and physical law and I thought that meant I should be a lawyer. I went Latin dancing at the Pirates restaurant in Orem. I dated Ali Clark, then Abby Cruz, and drove away from Abby one night in my rusted Honda CRX without much of an explanation.

In **winter 2012** I went to **Bolivia** on a BYU internship with my friend Matt Moen, teaching employment workshops through the national employment office. It remains one of the bright points of my life. I'm Andean on my mother's side, and I felt connected to my roots there. I lived on the eighth floor next to the church in **La Paz**, became close with our neighbors Lawrence and Heidi Chávez, who made me feel I belonged, and traveled to Potosí, Oruro, Tarija, and Sucre. I saw the Valle de la Luna and the Casa de la Moneda, got egged at Carnival, and danced until dawn in Oruro with a girl whose name I've lost. During Carnival we traveled north to Cobija, where my mother's sister took us across into Brazil. I met my cousins Luna, Simón, and Valentina, ate wild boar, and learned about Chico Mendes as we drove through Acre. A dermatologist in La Paz tried to laser away the redness in my face. I was always flushed. I think now it was shame.

That summer of 2012 I ran the **Deseret News Marathon in about 3 hours 33 minutes**. I met Nictehai Martínez, who made me eggs and went running with me and kissed me on her doorstep. I sometimes think I should have married her, then catch myself repeating my father's line about the woman *he* should have married.

### Marriage

I moved into a house we called Muffin Top with my friend Ian Ray and played music with him. In my new ward there was a girl I recognized from a dance back in Portland. I knocked on her door. She called me "bud." I was taken by her eyebrows. Her name was **Olivia**. We kissed on her parents' doorstep at Thanksgiving 2012. We wanted each other, the rules said wait, and the answer the culture offered was to marry fast. I spent that spring doing pirated Insanity workouts on a treadmill of expectation.

We were married in the temple in **June 2013**. The ceremony brought me to tears. My mother and Rich, the people who'd actually raised me, had to sit outside because they couldn't enter. We honeymooned at Disneyland and took a picture in front of the *happily ever after* mural with a smile I'd been practicing since grade school.

I wasn't ready to be anyone's husband. I worked for BYU's Office of IT, then spent six to eight months at a lab job building what was essentially an automated team-builder and produced nothing. I quit. I freelanced on a professor's website and did no work there either. I didn't know how to ask for help. I'd started smoking pot, first at school and then at home, passing out on the couch. I mistook dissociation for wisdom. I lied to Olivia about it, enjoyed getting away with it, and eventually brought her down into it with me. The one time I clearly remember us making love, in a hotel in eastern Oregon after getting high, I said cruel things in the middle of it. She laughed, as if it were a joke. It's still a sad core memory: what should have been tender was manipulative and frightened.

At a church meeting around then, a man stood up and said: *Stay the course. Don't deviate.* It hit me like a prophecy in reverse.

Olivia graduated in spring 2014. In **October 2014** we went back to Disneyland. At Halloween I was dressed as Thor, out of my mind, and I told her I wanted a divorce. I told her it was me, not her, that she'd always be my angel. A couple of months later she asked if she was still my angel, and I told her I'd made that up to please her. That was the last real conversation we had. We divorced that **November**. In 2015 I sent her an explicit email, and she told me never to contact her again. I sent one more from a secret address years later and got no reply. She's remarried now, to a man with a little daughter. My parents ran into her once at Nordstrom and were kind to her, because they are good people.

### Unraveling

After the divorce I stayed at BYU and told myself I'd graduate for my parents' sake. I didn't. I smoked in the bathroom and fell asleep at a library desk listening to Binary Star. I lived with Derek, Marcos, Muhammad, and others. I worked on the grounds crew and was a full-fledged pothead who thought he was sticking it to the institution. I took acid with Marcos and Derek and sat by the Provo River trying to find something calm. I smoked a pipe of Cavendish down by that river. My history capstone, on Native American history, was never finished. They gave me a C anyway.

That winter I checked out stacks of library books I never read, looking for an exit. In one I read about a young man with many personalities living inside him. In another, a Native American prayer book, I found the line that would carry me through the next decade: **in your greatest fear, look to one star.** In the religion stacks I found a pamphlet, *The Truth of the Rastafarians*. It told me I didn't have to throw out the baby with the bathwater, and it introduced me to Haile Selassie. Bob Marley's *Rastaman Vibration* had been in my blood since childhood. I've been a kind of clean-faced Rasta in spirit ever since.

In **summer 2015** my parents drove out in the gold Mercedes, put my bike on a new rack, and brought me home to Oregon. I kept up appearances on the drive. Rich could tell I wasn't okay.

### Long Island

In **October 2015** I bought a one-way ticket to an entrepreneurship conference in New York, telling myself I'd hitchhike home. I sat in the sessions listening to my own music, half believing it gave me powers. Afterward I was stranded at JFK for three or four days. I posted a GoFundMe, but nobody helped, because I acted as if everything was normal. I walked out onto the highway on the wrong side. Paramedics came and asked if I wanted to talk to someone about my mental health. I thought they meant a counselor. I ended up at **Long Island Jewish**, on the youth ward because I looked young. I told staff about what happened when I was five, and they listened. I treated the whole thing like another trip. I left with a psychiatric diagnosis in my records that no one ever sat me down to explain. Rich bought my ticket home.

That began what I think of as **ten years of psychiatric oversight, from 2015 to 2025**. My feelings about it are complicated and, I'll admit, not settled. Part of me believes I was mislabeled. Part of me admits I learned to work the system, saying what would get attention or shelter. Part of me knows I was genuinely lost.

### The Lost Years

Back home on Tumblestone Drive, I made my parents' lives hard. I smoked in my room, stole money from my brother, took substitute-teaching shifts, and contributed nothing. Rich and I got into a physical fight, him throwing me against a wall while trying to stay patient with a son who seemed too far gone. In **2016**, not really wanting to die but wanting to be seen, I hung myself from a thin rope, knowing Rich would come in. He pulled me down. Around then I also drank a bottle of NyQuil and threw it up. That year, after meeting a woman in the psych ward, I slept with her twice in a hotel. She was pregnant. It wasn't love. It was me looking for a trip. That same year I first heard of Falun Dafa.

I don't remember **2017**. I was probably in and out of hospitals. That's all I can honestly say.

### Hood River

In **January 2018** I moved back to Provo and worked at McDonald's and Cougar Pizza under a manager named Joe, who caught me lying and later reported me to the sober-living house when I smoked hash and drank after hours. My aunt in Nebraska took me in for the summer. Then I lived with my father, who'd remarried around 2013 and whose life was coming apart. I helped him load up to move back to his parents' house in Nebraska. In **Hood River**, I told him I was done, got out of the car, slammed the door, and flipped him off as he drove away. Some time after that, he was convicted of sexual contact with his stepdaughter, a minor, and he's now incarcerated, likely for the rest of his life. I don't have a tidy feeling about that. He called himself Dad, but when it came to protecting his son and teaching him how to deal with the world, he wasn't there.

I stayed in Hood River, homeless. I slept on a blanket in a gravel pit and made friends with other people living outside: Randall, Chase, Jay, and an old man whose name I've lost who only wanted enough alcohol to be fine for the day. Two skater kids, Elias and Riker, would come by to check on me. I panhandled and dumped out water bottles from in front of Safeway to return for joint money. It's one of the most shameful stretches of my life. Then I tried to steal eight dollars of stuff from Walmart, including a Halloween mask, and spent **about ten days in the Wasco County jail** around Halloween 2018. I asked to be moved to the psych unit because it was less scary. I took a plea. They bought me a bus ticket, and the first thing I did on getting out was find a joint.

Back in Hillsboro I moved in with a man called Douglas, who I'd later learn was really named Will Mullins. Late that year I used an online service to **remove my name from the records of the Mormon church**. In Douglas's mirror, I saw my own face cross-eyed and unfamiliar, and I was terrified. I truly believed then that I was under Satan's power. The temple had told me I would be if I broke my covenants, and I had. I believed it so completely that I threw my life away in degrees.

I turned twenty-nine in September 2018. The Open Field was supposed to be my launch. I didn't launch. I just kept lying to myself about who I was.

## IV. The True Reckoning
*2019–present · Ages 30–37*

### 2019: The Lot

I worked from January to November 2019 as a lot guy at **Royal Moore**, a certified pre-owned dealership. I had a paycheck and spent it at Goodwill, filling a storage unit with random things I called treasure. That was the year I found the teachings of **Mooji** and Advaita, which I'd first touched in 2015 through Dennis Waite's *The Book of One*: *there is nothing to fix, nothing wrong, only the vast presence of here and now.* I lived with Douglas, then with a young woman named Yarrow in Aloha, until she'd had enough of my beer bottles on her fire pit. Then I lived with Mario, whose father was from Sonora. There I isolated so badly I stopped using the bathroom. I also painted: feng shui canvases nailed to a tree in the woods, in the rain, as if everything were fine. I turned thirty that September.

### 2020: The Walk

I ended up homeless again. I spent two weeks in **Las Vegas** walking up and down town with a walking stick across my back like a cross, taking Christ's words literally without believing in him: *take up your cross and walk.* In January I went on to **Arizona** to be near my grandmother. I was homeless for about a month there and sometimes ate out of garbage cans. I called a mental health service and found its owner shared my first and middle names: Justin David Lee Harvey, of Just One Life. He helped steady me for a few months as COVID arrived. I worked as a janitor at Barnes & Noble and lived in a backyard unit behind Kevin Crosby's house until I made changes to it I had no right to make. Twice I tried to reach Mormon Lake, once walking and once on a bike, and failed both times. I left behind everything I'd hauled down from that storage unit. That walk away from my stuff was a purification, and I don't buy random things anymore.

In **April 2020** I came back to Oregon, tried living with Douglas again, and was out within weeks. Then I started walking.

I walked to **Tillamook**, about four days on fourteen dollars of food with one day of fasting, then down the coast toward Newport, aiming for Los Angeles. I walked for months, up to eighteen hours a day, on food stamps, feeling like a criminal because a man walking between towns without money is treated like one. Every town I entered seemed to go on edge, like in a movie when a stranger rides in. A young woman named **Shiloh** gave me a ride near Honeyman State Park. I met a man who had no arms. At night I cut my blanket down to a scrap because when you walk that much you just fall down and sleep.

In a teepee at Fort Bragg I heard a loud, roaring sound, like a big animal, and was terrified. It happened again in Trinidad, after a PhD student named Tim Epps gave me his fish sweater and a ride. I slept curled up inside that sweater with the torso pulled down over my legs. I heard the same roar a third time after I got home, out by my parents' shed. I believe it happened. I still don't know what it was.

I made it to **Marin County**, near San Rafael and Corte Madera, and couldn't find the Golden Gate Bridge. Whatever had been pushing me forward ran out there. I turned back north. Near **Brookings** I collapsed by the side of the road and was taken in by ambulance. The doctor there, Dr. Flowers, didn't call me mad. She said I was exhausted. A woman in Brookings gave me an old blanket when I left. I took a bus to Coos Bay, where my mother bought my ticket to Eugene and home. In **December 2020** I went back to Arizona.

### 2021: Three States

I stayed at a men's center in Mesa, worked at McDonald's again, and moved into a house of older men: Leroy, Jim, Pascual, and eventually a gravedigger named Viper, who moved into my room. Around May I quit and was back on the streets.

A mission acquaintance, Enrique Gómez, asked how I was doing and, when I told him, invited me to his tiny house in **Steamboat Springs, Colorado**. I trained jiu-jitsu, worked at Brooklyn's Pizza, and was hospitalized once there. After a frightening stretch in Denver, and with conflict over sobriety, I left in September as the cold came in.

In **Utah**, homeless in Springville (where an uncle lives, though we didn't have the kind of relationship that would have let me ask), I bought a **wheelbarrow**, painted it blue, chalked words on it, loaded it with a cinder block and the landscaping tools from my last paycheck, and pushed it north toward Tooele in honor of a real long-distance wheelbarrow walker. A man at a closed bank called the cops on me, and I had to come out of a 7-Eleven and answer their questions. I could see, in real time, how fear moves through a person's face when a stranger doesn't fit. A stranger gave me a ride for part of the way. In **Tooele** I stayed in a transitional house with four or five snoring men and couldn't stand it. They took my wheelbarrow, which broke my heart a little. Two social workers, Christy and Nadine, stayed in touch with me and bought my bus ticket home.

I moved back in with my mother and Rich on Shadow Lane. In October 2021 I went to a cousin's wedding.

### 2022–2023: Floor, Factory, Hut

In **2022** I slept on my mother's floor and worked at McDonald's, then the **Old Spaghetti Factory**. I held it together for a while, then went back to soothing myself with a weed vape. I took my parents' car to the smoke shop without asking. When Rich asked me why, I couldn't answer. From **September 2022 to January 2023** I washed dishes and helped with harvest at a **butcher shop** in Hillsboro.

In **April 2023** I had **LASIK** at NVision in Beaverton. After twenty years of glasses and contacts, it felt like starting over. Not long after, frustrated and not liking myself, I threw a backpack in my mother's house and she told me I wasn't welcome anymore.

I wandered Hillsboro. An old friend, Madison Wellington, whom I ran into at a farmers market, gave me a hammock and a key to his workshop. I ate all five boxes of his Girl Scout cookies, which I still feel bad about. I sawed up a fallen tree near the Costco on Cornell with his machete and hauled the branches with ropes, berserk. A local contractor, **Adrian Roman**, saw me and put me to work digging and painting alongside his guys, Abel and Arturo. Abel always said *perfectamundo*. Adrian let me sleep on his deck for a while, until I drifted off. I washed walls for about fifty dollars at Andy Karl Soelberg's place. I found an **abandoned hut** and lived in it for months, until I burned blackberry brush in broad daylight on a public trail. I was charged with trespass and arson, and the arson was dropped. I had to appear in court four or five times, spending money I didn't have to get there, and I played the madman in front of a public defender I held in contempt. I'm honestly not sure how that case ended. It's on my list to find out.

I visited churches for help and for something more. One night I carried three or four heavy iron railroad plates in a backpack to the Mormon church, as a kind of devotion. They didn't appreciate it, or what I was saying about polygamy.

Sometime around the holidays, my mother let me back into her world, this time into her **backyard**.

### 2024: Backyard, Storage Unit, Attic

I lived in that backyard from late 2023 into the fall of 2024, with one more journey in **February 2024**. I headed for Tillamook again, getting rides from Dimitri and a Christian named Coyote, then took the junction toward Roseburg. In **Florence** at 4 a.m. I met a woman I first mistook for a child. I kissed her. We were moved along from a laundromat, and I watched her smoke something in a park and noticed how hollow her bones seemed. In **Roseburg** I was completely spent. I called Justin David Lee Harvey, who bought me a ticket home, and a man named Greg Fort paid for a hotel that night. It saved me.

Back in the backyard by May, I barely left. I used a bucket and carried it to the dumpster every few days, ashamed of being seen. Justin sent me an iPhone, and I made AI art, made plans, and built a spreadsheet of my life from birth to fifty-five. I talked out loud to myself, a kind of stand-up, and to my parents it sounded like mania. They set me up in a **storage unit** with a cot. On the second day the attendant saw me on camera and told me to get out.

Around Halloween I talked my way into the wood shop of **Rob Hinton**, an old Scout leader. I resented that they had a big house and gave me a garage. I said something to his wife ("you're going to have to die") that she took as a threat, and he told me to leave. Instead I bolt-cut the lock on the shop's attic hatch and hid up there for a couple of days in primal fear before I came to my senses. My friend **Andy Silver** let me stay in his backyard, and I lived there from **November 2024 to June 2025**.

That November I made what I call *the good bad deal*: I accepted psychiatric oversight, including a long-acting injectable antipsychotic and about a year of appointments with a clinician, Dr. Richards. I was in and out of LifeWorks NW, Sequoia Mental Health, and the Hawthorne Walk-In Clinic. I did it partly because of where I was living and who I was depending on. I did it, I told myself, with no hope at all, which was also a kind of hope.

### 2025: Capitan, a Job, a Room

Early in 2025 I left Andy's for Phoenix, chasing a program that puts ex-offenders into trucking careers. My care team didn't want me to go. Justin didn't rescue me the way I'd hoped. The Phoenix shelter felt like a war zone and I left. At a Pentecostal revival in Mesa they fed me and bought me a sleeping bag. A barista at the Mesa library told me I should go to the hospital. Justin got me an Uber there, and I was transferred to Scottsdale, where they put me on a second antipsychotic on top of the injection. After about twelve days my **grandmother** picked me up. It was the last year of her life. For the first time, I really spent time with her: dinners, walks, her folk-magic Mormon house full of crystals and trinkets. I'm grateful for that.

Then I missed my bus. I believed a stranger who said it had already come, then watched it go by from the Taco Bell. I tore myself apart. Justin bought me another ticket. In the mountains north of **Sacramento** the bus hit snow in the pass and stalled, with talk of turning back. I got off with my one bag and walked up the road past a line of stopped trucks, knocking on doors. Finally a trucker named **Valerie**, maybe twenty-seven, who told me to call him *Capitán*, opened his door, fed me, let me smoke, played music, and drove me practically to my doorstep in Hillsboro. A year earlier I'd gone south dreaming of becoming a trucker. Instead a trucker carried me home.

I went hard on the job search. In **April 2025** I started at **Dick's Hillsboro Hyundai**, moving cars and doing whatever farmhand work the lot needs. Apart from about three months in the summer of 2025, I've worked there full time since.

In **June 2025** I got my own room. A man had died in it, and his belongings came with it. It took me about a year to clear them out. I scrubbed it top to bottom and ran a charcoal bar of soap and a line of salt around the edges, the way I'd done on my mother's patio. It's folk magic, salt to keep the slugs out. It also gave me hope.

On **July 20, 2025**, I wrote a **Certificate of Sovereign Being**. In **December 2025** I declined to renew the six-month injection and ended ten years of psychiatric oversight. My honest view is this: the medicine, for me, mostly made me tired and heavy. Healthcare too often doesn't care. A label can turn something that might be an opening into a life sentence. I also know I'm not the best judge of my own mind, and that the people who love me worry. I made the choice anyway, and I'm living it.

### 2026: The Bill of Intention

This year I quit the THC gummies and the drinking. I didn't frame it as a problem, just as a decision that they don't add to my life. I haven't quit cigarettes or the vape yet.

I've bounced between belief systems my whole life: the Mormonism of my father's side and my grandmothers' folk magic with its *signs and tokens*, Rastafari, Advaita and Mooji, A.C. Grayling's humanist *The Good Book*, and Falun Dafa, which I dove into hard this year and then stepped back from. I've gone back to Saturday meetings lately, because *truth, compassion, forbearance* is a good three-spoke wheel. I've also started learning **Nonviolent Communication** through Rose City NVC, learning to say what I feel and what I need.

What I've finally concluded is that I don't need an external system. So in **July and August 2026**, with AI's help, I wrote my own: ***The Bill of Intention: The Unified Operating Structure***, a seven-article covenant, alongside a graphic framework I call ***The Way of the Wheel***. The wheel has six spokes (body, food, time, home, other people, and the one I live with), and when all six turn, something good happens. My father, I realize, has lived a long time without a fixed framework. I shared that until I branched off. The difference is that I've written mine down.

There's been more this year, and not all of it flattering:

- In July I came out to my mother as gay over the phone. I knew while I said it that I was trying it on. I put on makeup and went on Grindr, and I came to understand I'm not. I learned something about how quickly people will hand you an identity if you don't know your own.
- I spent around three hundred dollars over two years on porn sites and apps full of fake profiles, and fell for a **romance scam** run from a Nigerian number posing as a woman in Ponca City, Oklahoma. I bought Apple gift cards for it. I came through safely. I haven't had sex in about nine years. I'm trying to understand that hunger without letting shame run it.
- At work I started making the old temple "first penalty" gesture, a hand drawn across the throat, as private resistance. A coworker thought it was a death threat. When my boss asked, I denied it. I was technically correct and wrong in every way that matters. I should have owned it and explained.
- I told my mother I was quitting to start a walking business. She cried, Rich called with his talking points, and I stayed.
- I clashed with a salesman and told him I wanted to punch him. That night I dreamed I was watching us from above.

I make eighteen dollars an hour. I'm thirty-seven, and some days I feel like I'm finishing last among my peers. I owe about twenty thousand dollars in student loans. I'd rather be a maverick truck driver working on my projects on the side. When I was little I heard my last name and thought it said *Maverick*. My uncle says nobody likes a maverick.

On **September 29, 2026**, I sat down to write about the Storm Years and realized I'm living them now. Everything I should have been wrestling with at thirteen, I'm wrestling with at thirty-seven. I feel like Ra Paulette carving his sandstone caves in New Mexico, except I'm Aladdin with the cave sealed, because I couldn't keep my hands off the treasure. There's so much sand and so little vision. Some days I just sit there, blue and purple.

And yet. Lately my mind, body, and heart have been working together better than they ever have. I notice when I physically feel unsafe, and I'm learning that when I feel unsafe, I probably am, and that I can still be strong, sure, and grounded. I end my days with forbearance. There's nothing to fix. There's nothing wrong. I look at the western sky.

## V. The Deepening & VI. The Long View
*Still ahead*

These pages are blank because I haven't lived them yet. I've left myself some notes for when I get there:

> Midlife is not a plateau; it is a vantage point. You can see more from here than from the valley floor. You have enough past to pattern-match from and enough future to act into. The grief of midlife (for youth, for possibility, for people lost) is real and should not be bypassed.

What that stage will ask of me:

- **Take your health seriously now.**
- **Pass something on.**
- **Redefine success.**
- **Welcome complexity.**
- **Make peace with unlived lives.**

If the Storm Years came late for me, maybe the rest will come late too, and that's all right. A late harvest is still a harvest. This archive is the map I'm building for whoever comes after: a *luminous map of the hurts of the past*, so they aren't wasted.
